INTRODUCTION
Food cost is one of the most important controllable expenses in a restaurant.
When food cost is controlled, the restaurant has a better chance to protect profit, keep pricing stable, reduce waste,
improve ordering decisions, and hold managers accountable for daily decisions.
When food cost is not controlled, the problems usually do not appear all at once.
They show up slowly.
A cook over-portions an expensive item.
A prep cook makes too much product.
A manager orders without checking what is already in the building.
Product expires in the walk-in because no one rotated it correctly.
A case is received but never checked against the invoice.
Employees throw food away without writing it down.
A recipe changes during the shift because someone is guessing.
A high-cost ingredient is used carelessly because no one explained its impact.
Each mistake may seem small by itself.
Together, those mistakes can cost the restaurant thousands of dollars over time.
Food cost control is not just about counting inventory.
It is a system.
A restaurant needs clear ordering habits, accurate receiving, strong storage organization, correct labeling, proper rotation,
realistic prep planning, recipe discipline, portion control, waste tracking, manager review, and employee accountability.
This workbook was created to help restaurant managers build that system.
The Food Cost, Waste & Inventory Control Workbook is a practical tool for full-service restaurants, casual dining
restaurants, bar and grill operations, steakhouses, independent restaurants, family restaurants, and restaurant teams that
need stronger control over food cost, waste, inventory, prep, ordering, and product accountability.
This is not a theory book.
This is a working workbook.
Inside this workbook, managers will find checklists, trackers, review pages, logs, training forms, par level builders,
inventory sheets, waste review tools, ordering worksheets, prep planning pages, portion control audits, and manager
accountability forms.
The goal is simple:
Know what is in the building.
Know what is being ordered.
Know what is being wasted.
Know what is being overproduced.
Know what is being underused.
Know what is being over-portioned.
Know what product movement cannot be explained.
Know what needs manager follow-up.
Food cost does not improve because managers hope it improves.
Food cost improves when managers track the right things, review the right patterns, train the right standards, and follow
up consistently.
A restaurant cannot control what it does not measure.
This workbook gives managers a place to start.


HOW TO USE THIS WORKBOOK
This workbook is designed for restaurant owners, general managers, assistant managers, kitchen managers, chefs, prep
leads, bar managers, inventory managers, and shift leaders who are responsible for food cost, waste, ordering, prep
planning, storage, inventory, or product control.
It can be used in several ways:
To build a weekly inventory system
To create stronger ordering routines
To reduce food waste
To track spoilage and expired product
To improve prep planning
To control portion sizes
To train employees on food cost awareness
To review high-cost ingredients
To identify overproduction and underproduction
To reconcile inventory with ordering, waste, production, transfers, and sales
To identify missing or inaccurate waste and production records
To investigate repeated unexplained product variance fairly
To hold managers accountable for food cost decisions
This workbook should not sit in an office unused.
It should be used during inventory, ordering, receiving, prep planning, kitchen meetings, manager reviews, food cost
follow-up, employee coaching, and weekly operations planning.
Food cost control is not one task.
It is a routine.
A restaurant should use this workbook weekly to review inventory, ordering, prep, waste, portion control, and product
movement.
Recommended Control Rhythm
Daily: Record waste, spoilage, remakes, comps, transfers, production quantities, prep yields, and unusual product use
while the information is still accurate.
Weekly: Count high-dollar and high-risk products; compare inventory movement with ordering, receiving, waste,
production, sales, portions, and transfers; and assign follow-up for unexplained differences.
Monthly: Complete a full inventory, calculate food used and food-cost percentage, review variance patterns, verify the
accuracy of supporting records, and correct the system before the next month begins.
For best results, use this workbook in this order:
Start with the food cost foundation pages.
Set up inventory categories and storage areas.
Build the inventory count system.
Create par levels for major products.
Review ordering habits.
Track waste every day.
Review portion control for high-cost items.
Use prep planning pages before production begins.
Audit storage, labeling, and rotation.
Review results weekly with managers.
Do not try to fix every food cost problem at once.
Start with the biggest leak.
That may be over-ordering.
It may be waste.
It may be poor prep planning.
It may be over-portioning.
It may be untracked remakes.
It may be expired product.

It may be managers ordering without accurate counts.
Once the biggest issue is visible, the manager can assign action, set a deadline, train the team, and follow up.
The purpose of this workbook is not paperwork.
The purpose is control.
Food cost control becomes stronger when the restaurant can answer these questions:
What did we buy?
What did we use?
What did we waste?
What did we prep?
What did we throw away?
What did we overproduce?
What did we run out of?
What did we count incorrectly?
What did managers fail to review?
When those questions are answered consistently, the restaurant becomes easier to manage.
Clear numbers create better decisions.
Better decisions protect profit.

WHY FOOD COST GETS OUT OF CONTROL
Food cost usually gets out of control because small daily habits are not being tracked.
Most restaurants do not lose control of food cost from one single mistake.
They lose control because the same small mistakes happen again and again.
A manager orders too much product because the walk-in was not counted.
A cook uses a bigger portion than the recipe allows.
A prep employee makes a full batch when a half batch was enough.
A case of product gets pushed behind newer product and expires.
A server rings in the wrong item and the kitchen remakes food.
A bartender or cook gives away product without documentation.
A food item is returned by a guest but never logged.
A manager sees waste but does not ask why it happened.
A product is low, so someone places an emergency order at a higher cost.
A supplier invoice changes, but no one checks the price.
A restaurant cannot fix food cost only by telling employees to “waste less.”
Waste less is not a system.
Order better is not a system.
Watch portions is not a system.
A system gives managers a way to see what is happening.
Food cost problems usually come from one or more of these areas:
Poor inventory counts
Weak ordering habits
No par levels
No waste log
No portion standards
Poor recipe training
Overproduction
Expired product
Poor storage rotation
Unclear receiving process
No manager follow-up
No employee accountability
The first step is not blame.
The first step is visibility.
Managers need to know where the loss is coming from before they can correct it.
A restaurant may believe its biggest problem is high supplier pricing, but the real problem may be overproduction.
Another restaurant may believe its biggest problem is waste, but the real problem may be incorrect ordering.
Another restaurant may believe employees are careless, but the real problem may be that portion tools were never
assigned or recipe standards were never trained.
Food cost control starts when the restaurant stops guessing.
The manager’s job is to identify the leak, document the pattern, train the correction, and follow up until the standard
improves.

THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN SALES, COST, WASTE, AND PROFIT
Food cost control becomes easier when managers understand the difference between sales, cost, waste, and profit.
These words are often used together, but they do not mean the same thing.
Sales are the dollars the restaurant brings in from guests.
Cost is the money spent to buy the food, ingredients, supplies, and products needed to produce those sales.
Waste is product the restaurant paid for but did not turn into profitable sales.
Profit is what remains after costs are controlled and the restaurant protects enough margin to stay healthy.
A restaurant can have strong sales and still lose profit if food cost is uncontrolled.
High sales do not automatically mean strong performance.
A busy restaurant can still waste too much product, over-order, over-prep, over-portion, miss invoice changes, throw away
expired food, remake too many items, or give away food without tracking it.
That is why managers must look beyond sales.
Sales answer one question:
How much money did guests spend?
Food cost answers a different question:
How much did it cost the restaurant to produce those sales?
Waste answers another question:
How much product did the restaurant pay for but fail to use correctly?
Profit answers the most important question:
After the restaurant served guests and paid its costs, what was actually protected?
A manager who only watches sales may miss the bigger problem.
For example, a restaurant may sell a large number of steaks on a weekend.
That sounds good.
But if cooks over-portion sides, trim product poorly, remake plates, throw away unused prep, and fail to track waste, the
profit from those steak sales may be much lower than expected.
Another restaurant may have average sales but excellent portion control, tight prep, accurate ordering, low waste, and
strong inventory review.
That restaurant may protect better profit than a busier restaurant with weaker systems.
Food cost control is not about being cheap.
It is about making sure paid product is used correctly.
Guests should still receive good portions, quality food, and a strong experience.


The goal is not to take value away from the guest.
The goal is to stop losing money through avoidable mistakes.
A manager should review food cost decisions with this mindset:
Did we buy the right amount?
Did we receive what we paid for?
Did we store it correctly?
Did we rotate it correctly?
Did we prep the right amount?
Did we portion it correctly?
Did we sell it before it spoiled?
Did we track what was wasted?
Did we learn from the waste?
Did we follow up with the team?
Food cost control is not one number.
It is the result of many daily decisions.
When managers understand the difference between sales, cost, waste, and profit, they can make better decisions before
the damage grows.

WHAT MANAGERS MUST TRACK EVERY WEEK
Food cost control works best when managers track the same important areas every week.
A restaurant cannot depend on memory.
A manager may believe waste was low, but the waste log may show repeated spoilage.
A manager may believe ordering was accurate, but the walk-in may show too much backup product.
A manager may believe portion control is strong, but a high-cost ingredient may be disappearing faster than sales can
explain.
Tracking gives managers facts.
Facts make better decisions possible.
A restaurant does not need to make food cost tracking complicated.
But it does need to be consistent.
The most important weekly tracking areas are:
Inventory counts
Weekly high-dollar and high-risk counts
Monthly full inventory
Food purchases
Supplier invoices
Transfers in and out
Product price changes
Waste
Spoilage
Remakes and comps
Prep and production records
Par levels
Overproduction
Underproduction
Portion control
High-cost ingredients
Inventory variance
Storage organization
Labeling and dating
Manager follow-up
Each area tells part of the story.
Inventory counts show what is actually in the building.
Food purchases show what the restaurant bought.
Supplier invoices show what the restaurant paid.
Product price changes show where cost may be increasing.
Waste logs show what was lost.
Spoilage logs show what was not used in time.
Remake logs show where mistakes may be happening.
Prep reviews show whether the restaurant is making too much or too little.
Portion audits show whether recipes are being followed.
Storage checks show whether product is being protected.
Manager follow-up shows whether problems are being corrected or ignored.
The weekly review does not have to be perfect at first.
It has to begin.

A manager should start by choosing the areas that are causing the most money loss.
If waste is high, start with daily waste logs.
If product keeps expiring, start with storage, labeling, and rotation.
If food cost is high but waste looks low, review portion control and invoice prices.
If the restaurant keeps running out of product, review par levels and ordering.
If too much product is being prepped, review sales patterns and production planning.
The goal is to create a weekly rhythm.
When managers review the same things every week, patterns become easier to see.
A pattern is more useful than a single mistake.
One wasted pan of product may be a one-time problem.
The same product wasted three weeks in a row is a system problem.
One emergency order may be unavoidable.
Emergency orders every week show weak planning.
One over-portioned plate may be a training issue.
Over-portioning every shift shows a lack of recipe control.
Weekly tracking helps managers stop reacting and start managing.

CORE FOOD COST AND VARIANCE CALCULATIONS
Managers do not need complicated accounting language to use these calculations. They do need consistent counts, the
same units of measure, complete receiving records, and honest supporting logs.
Food Used
Beginning Inventory + Purchases + Transfers In - Transfers Out - Ending Inventory = Food Used
Food-Cost Percentage
Food Used ÷ Food Sales × 100 = Food-Cost Percentage
Unexplained Inventory Variance
Product Available - Product Accounted For = Unexplained Variance
A variance is a signal that something needs to be checked. It is not proof of theft, misconduct, or intentional
wrongdoing.

The manager should compare the number with sales and recipe usage, receiving, waste, production, transfers, remakes,
comps, samples, storage, portions, and count accuracy before reaching a conclusion. Record the resulting variance here
only after completing the item-level reconciliation in Part 2.

HOW SMALL MISTAKES BECOME BIG FOOD COST PROBLEMS
Most food cost problems begin small.
That is what makes them dangerous.
A small mistake may not seem worth correcting in the moment.
A cook uses a little too much cheese.
A server forgets to ring in an extra sauce.
A prep cook makes one extra pan of product.
A manager accepts a delivery without checking the invoice.
A container goes unlabeled in the walk-in.
A case gets placed in front of older product.
An employee throws away food without recording it.
A bartender over-pours slightly.
A manager notices but decides to deal with it later.
One small mistake may not damage the restaurant.
Repeated small mistakes can become a major cost problem.
Food cost is often lost through repetition.
A few ounces over-portioned every plate becomes pounds of product lost by the end of the week.
One untracked remake becomes a pattern of kitchen mistakes that no one studies.
One unlabeled container becomes a habit that leads to expired product.
One missed invoice check becomes weeks of unnoticed price increases.
One manager skipping waste review teaches the team that waste does not matter.
Small mistakes become big problems when they are ignored, repeated, normalized, or left undocumented.
Managers must pay attention to small problems because small problems are easier to correct early.
It is easier to correct portion control during training than to explain why food cost is high at the end of the month.
It is easier to fix labeling habits today than to throw away spoiled product next week.
It is easier to review ordering before the delivery arrives than to store too much product in an already crowded walk-in.
It is easier to log waste during the shift than to guess at the end of the week.
Food cost control depends on daily discipline.
Managers should not treat every small mistake like a crisis.
But managers should treat repeated mistakes like a system warning.
A repeated mistake means one of four things is happening:
The employee was not trained clearly.
The standard is not written down.
The manager is not following up.
The system is too weak to catch the problem.
Correcting small mistakes does not have to be harsh.
It should be clear.
A manager can say:
“This portion needs to match the recipe.”
“This product has to be labeled before it goes into the walk-in.”
“This waste needs to be written down.”
“This item cannot be ordered until we check what we already have.”
“This product needs to be rotated behind the older case.”
“This remake needs to be logged so we can see the pattern.”
The goal is not to blame employees.
The goal is to protect the restaurant.
When managers correct small food cost problems early, they prevent bigger problems later.

FOOD COST CONTROL WITHOUT HURTING GUEST EXPERIENCE
Food cost control should never be used as an excuse to damage the guest experience.
A restaurant can control cost and still serve quality food.
The goal is not to make plates look smaller, reduce quality, disappoint guests, or make employees afraid to serve
properly.
The goal is to serve the correct product, in the correct portion, using the correct recipe, with the correct standard, every
time.
Food cost control becomes harmful when managers confuse control with cutting corners.
Cutting corners may lower cost for a short time, but it can damage trust.
Guests notice when portions shrink without explanation.
Guests notice when quality drops.
Guests notice when recipes become inconsistent.
Guests notice when a favorite item looks different every visit.
Guests notice when a plate feels careless.
The right way to control food cost is to remove waste, not value.
A manager should protect value by controlling the parts of the process that should never have been loose in the first
place.
Examples include:
Using the recipe instead of guessing
Using the correct scoop, ladle, scale, or portion cup
Prepping based on realistic sales needs
Rotating product so older product is used first
Checking deliveries before accepting them
Recording waste when it happens
Training employees on high-cost ingredients
Reviewing remakes and returned food
Keeping storage areas organized
Adjusting par levels based on real usage
Following up when products are repeatedly wasted
None of those actions hurt the guest.
They protect the restaurant while still protecting the guest.
Good food cost control should make the guest experience more consistent.
If recipes are followed, guests receive the same item each visit.
If portions are correct, guests receive fair value and the restaurant protects margin.
If prep is planned well, the restaurant is less likely to run out of important items.
If product is rotated correctly, quality improves.
If waste is tracked, managers can fix problems before they affect guests.
Food cost control should be explained to employees carefully.
Employees should understand that the goal is not to be cheap.
The goal is to be consistent, responsible, and professional.
A cook should know that portion control protects both food cost and guest trust.
A prep cook should know that overproduction creates waste and underproduction creates service problems.

What product is on hand

How product is counted

STORAGE AREA MAP
A restaurant cannot count inventory accurately if managers and employees do not know where product belongs.
A storage area map helps the team create order.
It does not need to be fancy.
It needs to be clear.
A storage map shows where product categories should be kept so inventory can be counted, deliveries can be put away
correctly, product can be rotated, and employees can find what they need during the shift.
Without a storage map, product often moves around based on convenience.
Dry goods may be placed wherever there is space.
Frozen product may be stacked in front of older cases.
Bar product may be mixed with kitchen product.
Prep product may be stored in several places.
Sauces, dressings, proteins, produce, paper goods, chemicals, and backup supplies may end up in the wrong areas.
When that happens, inventory becomes harder to count.
Ordering becomes less accurate.
Waste becomes harder to see.
Employees open new product before using old product.
Managers order items that are already in the building.
Storage problems create food cost problems.
A storage area map helps prevent that.
Each major storage area should have a simple layout.
The map should show:
What belongs in the area
What does not belong in the area
Where high-cost products are stored
Where open product should go
Where backup product should go
Where damaged product should be reported
Where product should be staged after delivery
Where chemicals should never be stored
Where food safety risks may exist
A storage area map also helps with training.
New employees can learn where items belong.
Prep cooks can find product faster.
Cooks can restock more consistently.
Managers can inspect storage areas more easily.
Inventory counts become more repeatable.
A manager should review the storage map whenever the menu changes, storage layout changes, supplier packaging
changes, or inventory problems keep repeating.
The map should match the real restaurant.
If the walk-in has five shelves, the map should reflect those shelves.
If dry storage has zones, the map should show those zones.
If product is stored in multiple locations, the map should identify each location.
The purpose is not to make storage complicated.
The purpose is to make storage visible, organized, and easier to control.

UNIT OF MEASURE SETUP PAGE
Inventory accuracy depends on using the correct unit of measure.
A unit of measure explains how a product is counted.
If one manager counts a product by the case, another counts it by the pound, and another counts it by each item, the
inventory will not be consistent.
Inconsistent units create inaccurate counts.
Inaccurate counts create bad ordering decisions.
Bad ordering decisions create waste, shortages, emergency orders, overstocking, and food cost confusion.
A restaurant must decide how each product should be counted.
Common units of measure include:
Case
Bag
Box
Pound
Ounce
Gallon
Quart
Pint
Cup
Each
Bottle
Can
Container
Sleeve
Rack
Dozen
Pan
Tray
Portion
Batch
The correct unit depends on how the restaurant buys, stores, uses, and tracks the product.
For example, a case of chicken may be purchased by the case but used by the pound.
A sauce may be made by the batch but stored by the quart.
A dessert may be purchased by the case but sold by the slice.
A bottle of liquor may be purchased by the bottle but tracked by bottle count or partial bottle estimate.
A box of gloves may be counted by the box.
A sleeve of cups may be counted by the sleeve.
Managers should choose the unit that creates the most useful count.
The unit should be easy to understand.
It should be repeatable.
It should match the inventory sheet.
It should match ordering needs when possible.
It should be clear enough that a different manager could count the same product and get a similar result.
Some items may need two measurements:
Purchase unit
Inventory count unit


For example:
Purchased by case, counted by each package.
Purchased by case, counted by pound.
Purchased by bottle, counted by bottle.
Purchased by box, counted by sleeve.
The restaurant should document this before inventory begins.
If the unit is unclear, managers may estimate.
Estimating creates weak numbers.
Weak numbers create weak decisions.
A strong unit of measure system helps managers count inventory the same way every week.

INVENTORY COUNT SHEET
The inventory count sheet is one of the most important food cost control tools in the restaurant.
It tells managers what product is in the building.
It supports ordering decisions.
It helps identify overstocking.
It helps identify shortages.
It helps managers compare usage to sales.
It helps reveal waste, spoilage, and portion control problems.
But an inventory count sheet only works if it is organized and used consistently.
A weak count sheet creates confusion.
A strong count sheet creates clarity.
A good inventory count sheet should include:
Product name
Product category
Storage area
Unit of measure
Beginning count
Ending count
Amount ordered
Amount received
Waste or spoilage notes
Manager initials
Follow-up notes
Not every restaurant needs every column at first.
But every restaurant needs a count sheet that can be used the same way every week.
The count sheet should follow the storage flow.
That means the order of the sheet should match the way the manager walks through the restaurant.
If the manager starts in dry storage, dry storage should be first.
If the manager counts the walk-in next, the walk-in should come next.
If bar storage is counted separately, it should have its own section.
This helps prevent missed items.
Managers should avoid building a count sheet that jumps from category to category without matching the real building.
A good count sheet should also separate high-cost items.
High-cost products should receive extra attention because small mistakes can create larger cost problems.
A manager should count high-cost proteins, seafood, specialty products, alcohol, and expensive ingredients carefully.
Inventory counts should not be rushed.
A rushed count often becomes a guessed count.
A guessed count is not inventory control.
Managers should count at the same time each week when possible.
The count should happen before major ordering decisions.
The count should be reviewed before the order is placed.
The final question should always be:
Does this count make sense based on sales, prep, waste, and product movement?
If the answer is no, the manager should investigate before ordering.

OPENING INVENTORY SETUP
Opening inventory creates the starting point for the restaurant’s food cost control system.
Before managers can track progress, they need a beginning count.
The opening inventory count shows what is in the building at the start of the tracking period.
Without an opening inventory, managers may not know whether food cost improved, worsened, or stayed the same.
Opening inventory is especially important when a restaurant is starting a new food cost system, switching managers,
changing suppliers, updating menus, creating new par levels, or trying to solve ongoing waste problems.
The opening count should be careful.
It should not be guessed.
It should not be rushed.
It should not be done from memory.
A manager should count each storage area and document the products clearly.
If possible, high-cost products should be verified by a second manager.
Opening inventory should answer these questions:
What product is currently in the building?
Where is it stored?
How much is available?
What unit of measure is being used?
What product is expired or questionable?
What product is overstocked?
What product is low?
What product needs to be ordered soon?
What product needs to be used first?
What product needs manager follow-up?
Opening inventory also helps managers clean up the system before weekly tracking begins.
If old product is found, it should be reviewed.
If product is unlabeled, it should be corrected.
If product is stored in the wrong area, it should be moved.
If the count sheet is missing items, it should be updated.
If the unit of measure is unclear, it should be fixed.
The opening count is not just a number.

It is a reset.
It gives the restaurant a chance to begin the food cost system with better organization, better visibility, and better control.

WEEKLY INVENTORY ROUTINE
Inventory works best when it becomes a routine.
A restaurant should not count inventory only when food cost looks bad.
By the time food cost looks bad, the problem may already have been happening for weeks.
A weekly inventory routine gives managers a regular way to review product levels, ordering needs, storage problems,
waste concerns, and food cost risks.
The routine should be simple enough to repeat.
It should happen on the same day when possible.
It should use the same count sheets.
It should follow the same storage path.
It should use the same unit of measure.
It should include manager review before the order is placed.
A weekly inventory routine helps managers stop guessing.
Instead of asking, “Do we need more product?” the manager can look at the count.
Instead of asking, “Why did we run out?” the manager can review par levels, sales, prep, and ordering.
Instead of asking, “Why is food cost high?” the manager can compare inventory, purchasing, waste, and portion control.
Inventory should not be treated as a paperwork task.
It should be treated as a decision-making tool.
A strong weekly inventory routine should include:
Preparing the count sheets
Cleaning and organizing storage areas
Counting each storage area
Reviewing high-cost items
Checking labels and dates
Identifying expired or questionable product
Reviewing waste concerns
Comparing counts to par levels
Reviewing upcoming sales needs
Reviewing upcoming events or holidays
Reviewing order needs
Assigning follow-up actions
The count should be completed before major ordering decisions are made.
Ordering before counting is one of the fastest ways to create overstocking.
A manager who orders from memory may miss product that is already in the building.
A manager who orders without checking par levels may order too much or too little.
A manager who orders without reviewing waste may keep buying product that the restaurant is not using correctly.
Weekly inventory should create better questions.
Why are we carrying this much product?
Why did this item move so fast?
Why did this item not move?
Why did this product expire?
Why did we run out?
Why did we need an emergency order?
Why did this count not match expected usage?
Why was this product in the wrong storage area?
Those questions help managers improve the system.
The goal is not just to count.
The goal is to learn from the count.

INVENTORY ACCURACY REVIEW
Inventory is only useful if it is accurate.
An inaccurate inventory count can create more problems than no count at all because managers may trust bad
information.
If the count says there is enough product, the manager may not order more.
If the count says product is low when it is actually overstocked, the manager may order too much.
If the unit of measure is wrong, food cost reports may become confusing.
If storage areas are skipped, managers may believe product is missing when it is simply stored somewhere else.
Inventory accuracy depends on discipline.
Managers should not guess.
Managers should not count from memory.
Managers should not rush through storage areas.
Managers should not change units of measure without updating the count sheet.
Managers should not ignore products that are hard to count.
The inventory accuracy review helps managers find weak spots in the count process.
Common inventory accuracy problems include:
Products stored in multiple places
Products counted with different units
Partial cases estimated differently
Open containers not measured consistently
Items missing from the count sheet
Items counted twice
Items skipped
Products stored in the wrong area
Expired product included in usable inventory
Damaged product not removed
Waste not recorded
Deliveries counted before invoices are checked
Manager does not review unusual counts
Accuracy improves when the restaurant creates clear counting rules.
For example:
Count all unopened cases as full cases.
Count partial cases by the agreed unit.
Count open bottles according to the restaurant’s bottle-count method.
Do not count expired product as usable inventory.
Do not count damaged product as usable inventory.
Record questionable product for manager review.
Update the count sheet when a new product is added.
Remove discontinued products from the count sheet.
Count high-cost items twice if needed.
A manager should review inventory accuracy after each count.
The review does not need to be long.
It should answer one simple question:
Can we trust this count?
If the answer is no, the manager should fix the system before using the count to make ordering decisions.
Accurate inventory creates accurate ordering.
Accurate ordering protects food cost.

INVENTORY VARIANCE, RECORD ACCURACY, AND UNEXPLAINED
PRODUCT LOSS
Inventory is not only a count of what remains. When it is compared with ordering, receiving, transfers, waste, production,
portions, sales, remakes, and other approved uses, it becomes an early-warning system.
The numbers give the manager a reason to check. They do not give the manager permission to accuse.

Why This Review Matters
A shortage may come from a counting mistake, a delivery or invoice error, unrecorded waste, production that was not
written down, a transfer to another location that was never recorded, a remake or comp that was missed, poor portion
control, spoilage, misplaced product, recipe inconsistency, or possible theft. The first responsibility is to find the most
supportable explanation.
The same process also reveals when waste and production records are incomplete, inaccurate, or not being used at all. If
the physical count shows more product was used than the records explain, the manager knows where to begin checking
and retraining.
Required Counting Rhythm
Weekly: Count designated high-dollar, high-volume, short-shelf-life, and repeatedly missing products. The list should be
based on actual restaurant risk, not only price.
Monthly: Complete a full inventory of every storage area at least once each month, using the same item order and units of
measure each time. Recount unusual results before using them to make a decision.
Additional Counts: Recount any item with an unusual shortage, repeated variance, storage concern, receiving issue, or
major price change.
Product-Flow Reconciliation
Product Available = Beginning Inventory + Purchases or Receipts + Transfers In
Product Accounted For = Ending Inventory + Transfers Out + Expected Recipe Usage + Recorded
Waste + Approved Adjustments
Unexplained Variance = Product Available - Product Accounted For
Expected recipe usage should be calculated from POS sales counts and standardized recipes. Approved adjustments
may include documented remakes, comps, samples, employee meals, or yield and production adjustments that are not
already included in expected recipe usage, recorded waste, or ending inventory. Never count the same product movement
twice. Always compare using the same unit of measure. A case, pound, each, pan, bottle, ounce, or portion cannot be
mixed without conversion.
Fair Investigation Sequence
1. Recount the product and verify every storage location.
2. Check invoices, credits, receiving records, and units of measure.
3. Check transfers in and out, including loans to another restaurant or department.
4. Compare waste, spoilage, remake, comp, and returned-food records.
5. Compare production sheets, prep yields, batch records, and sales or recipe usage.
6. Review portion standards, recipe compliance, and unrecorded employee or manager use.
7. Correct missing records, retrain the process, and count again.
8. Escalate only when a repeated pattern remains unexplained after ordinary causes have been checked.
Repeated Patterns Matter
One variance may be a mistake. Repeated variance on the same item, shift, storage area, or record process is evidence
that the system needs deeper review. Pattern tracking helps the manager separate an isolated error from an ongoing
control failure.

PART 3
ORDERING CONTROL
Purpose of This Section
Ordering is one of the most important food cost control responsibilities in a restaurant.
A strong order protects the restaurant.
A weak order creates problems before the product even arrives.
Ordering too much can create overstocking, spoilage, waste, crowded storage areas, poor rotation, and unnecessary
spending.
Ordering too little can create shortages, emergency orders, menu outages, guest disappointment, prep problems, and
manager stress.
Ordering the wrong product can create recipe problems, quality problems, invoice problems, and product confusion.
Ordering without checking inventory is guessing.
Ordering without reviewing par levels is risky.
Ordering without reviewing sales patterns can create waste or shortages.
Ordering without checking upcoming events, reservations, holidays, weather, or specials can leave the restaurant
unprepared.
Ordering should not depend only on habit.
A manager should not order something simply because “we always order it.”
A manager should ask:
How much do we have?
How much do we normally use?
How much do we expect to sell?
What is already prepped?
What is close to expiration?
What is on special?
What is coming up this week?
What did we waste last week?
What did we run out of last week?
What product price changed?
What supplier issue should we expect?
Ordering control is not about under-ordering.
It is about ordering with facts.
The best restaurant orders are built from inventory counts, sales expectations, par levels, prep needs, storage limits,
waste review, and manager judgment.
A manager should also understand that ordering affects more than food cost.
It affects kitchen flow.
It affects prep planning.
It affects employee stress.
It affects guest experience.
It affects storage cleanliness.
It affects cash flow.
It affects waste.
When ordering is controlled, the restaurant runs smoother.
When ordering is careless, the restaurant pays for it all week.
ORDERING RESPONSIBILITY CHECKLIST

If everyone is responsible, no one is fully responsible.
A restaurant should know who builds the order, who reviews it, who approves it, who places it, who receives it, and who

One manager assumes another manager checked inventory.
A kitchen lead adds product without reviewing par levels.

Who places the supplier order?

Ordering responsibility may be shared, but accountability should not be vague.
A kitchen manager may build the food order.
A general manager may approve it.

A good order should be connected to expected sales.
Managers should not order only based on what was used last week.
They should also consider what is likely to happen this week.
Sales forecasting does not have to be perfect.
It needs to be thoughtful.
A restaurant should look at patterns that affect product usage.
Important forecasting factors include:
Last week’s sales
Same week last year if available
Day-of-week patterns
Reservations
Large parties
Catering or events
Holidays
Weather
Local events
Menu specials
Promotions
Seasonal items
Staffing levels
Product availability
Supplier delivery schedule
For example, a rainy week may reduce patio sales.
A holiday weekend may increase sales.
A large reservation may increase steak, seafood, dessert, or wine usage.
A new special may increase demand for a specific ingredient.
A local event may increase late-night sales.
A short-staffed kitchen may reduce what the restaurant can realistically execute.
A supplier delivery delay may require ordering more before the next delivery.
Ordering should match the restaurant’s expected need.
Without a forecast, managers often order from habit.
Habit ordering may work during average weeks.
It may fail during unusual weeks.
A manager should review the upcoming week before placing the order.
The question is not just, “What are we low on?”
The better question is:
“What will we need based on what is coming?”
A forecast helps managers avoid both shortages and over-ordering.
It also helps prep planning.
If the restaurant expects a high-volume weekend, prep levels should be adjusted.
If the restaurant expects a slower week, prep should be controlled to avoid waste.
Sales forecasting is not about predicting perfectly.
It is about preparing better.

PAR LEVEL BUILDER
Par levels help managers decide how much product the restaurant should normally keep on hand.
A par level is the target amount of product needed to operate without overstocking or running out.
Without par levels, managers often order based on habit, fear, memory, or guesswork.
That creates problems.
If par levels are too high, the restaurant may carry too much product.
Too much product can lead to spoilage, crowded storage areas, poor rotation, unnecessary spending, and higher waste.
If par levels are too low, the restaurant may run out of product.
Running out can lead to emergency orders, menu outages, guest disappointment, rushed prep, manager stress, and poor
service.
A strong par level should be based on real usage.
Managers should consider:
Average weekly sales
Average product usage
Delivery schedule
Shelf life
Storage space
Menu popularity
Seasonal demand
Special events
Waste patterns
Prep time
Supplier reliability
Backup needs
Par levels should not be permanent.
They should be reviewed and adjusted when the restaurant changes.
A par level may need to change when:
Sales increase
Sales decrease
A menu item becomes more popular
A menu item slows down
A supplier changes pack size
A product has repeated spoilage
A product runs out repeatedly
A holiday or event is coming
Weather changes sales patterns
A new menu item is added
A product is discontinued
Par levels are especially important for high-cost items.
A restaurant should not carry too much expensive product unless sales support it.
High-cost products should be reviewed carefully before ordering.
Examples include steaks, seafood, specialty cheese, premium produce, specialty desserts, liquor, wine, and any
ingredient with a short shelf life.
A par level should help the manager answer:
How much should we have before ordering?
How much do we need to get through the next delivery period?
How much is too much?
How much is too little?
What amount protects both guest experience and food cost?
The best par levels are practical.
They are not random numbers.
They are based on actual restaurant needs.

ORDER GUIDE TEMPLATE
An order guide helps managers place orders consistently.
It lists the products the restaurant normally buys, the supplier, the storage area, the unit of measure, the par level, and the
amount that should be ordered.
A strong order guide reduces missed items, duplicate ordering, over-ordering, and confusion.
Without an order guide, managers may depend on memory.
Memory is not a reliable ordering system.
A manager may forget a product.
A manager may order an old product that is no longer used.
A manager may order too much because the par level is unclear.
A manager may miss a supplier change.
A manager may forget that a product is already stored in another area.
An order guide should match the restaurant’s actual menu and storage system.
It should be updated when products change.
If a new menu item is added, the order guide should be updated.
If a product is discontinued, the order guide should be updated.
If a supplier changes pack size, the order guide should be updated.

OVER-ORDERING RISK TRACKER
Over-ordering is one of the easiest ways to lose food cost control.
It often happens with good intentions.
A manager does not want to run out.
A cook asks for extra backup.
A prep lead wants to be safe.
A holiday is coming.
A supplier has a deal.
A manager remembers a busy week and orders heavily again.
The problem is that extra product does not always turn into extra sales.
Extra product can turn into waste.
Over-ordering creates several risks:
Spoilage
Expired product
Crowded walk-ins
Poor rotation
Hidden product
Cash tied up in inventory
More difficult counting
More product opened unnecessarily
More waste when sales are lower than expected
More manager confusion
Over-ordering can also hide other problems.
If managers always order extra, they may not notice poor prep planning.
If there is always too much product, employees may not treat product carefully.
If storage is crowded, FIFO becomes harder.
If product is hidden behind too much backup, waste increases.
The goal is not to order too little.
The goal is to order the right amount.
Managers should watch for over-ordering risks before placing an order.
Common warning signs include:
Current count is already above par
Product did not sell as expected last week
Product has short shelf life
Product was wasted recently
Product is already prepped in large amounts
Storage area is crowded
Product is used in only one menu item
Product is slow-moving
Product price is high
Product is being ordered because of habit
Product is being ordered because someone is nervous
Product has been expiring before use
When these warning signs appear, the manager should pause.
The manager should ask:
Do we really need this product?
Can we use what we already have first?
Should we reduce the order?
Should we adjust prep instead?
Should we wait until the next delivery?
Should this product be reviewed by the GM or owner before ordering?

Over-ordering is preventable when managers review before they buy.

PART 4
WASTE TRACKING AND REDUCTION
Purpose of This Section
Waste is one of the clearest signs of food cost loss.
When product is thrown away, spoiled, remade, over-prepped, burned, dropped, mislabeled, expired, returned, or
portioned incorrectly, the restaurant has already paid for it.
If that loss is not tracked, the restaurant may keep repeating the same mistake.
Waste does not improve when managers ignore it.
Waste improves when managers see it, record it, review it, train against it, and follow up.
Many restaurants throw away product without writing it down.
That creates a blind spot.
The manager may know that waste happens, but not how much, how often, where it happens, who is involved, or what
product is most affected.
A waste system helps the restaurant answer:
What was wasted?
How much was wasted?
Why was it wasted?
Where did it happen?
Who reported it?
Was it preventable?
What action should be taken?
Is this a repeated problem?
Waste is not always caused by carelessness.
Waste can come from poor ordering, poor prep planning, poor labeling, weak rotation, unclear recipes, over-portioning,
equipment problems, guest complaints, training gaps, or management decisions.
That is why waste tracking should not be used only to blame employees.
It should be used to improve the system.
Managers should track several types of waste:
Prep waste
Cook line waste
Spoilage
Expired product
Dropped product
Burned product
Wrong item made
Returned food
Remakes
Overproduction
Trim waste
Bar waste
Product damaged during receiving
Product damaged during storage
A strong waste system helps managers focus.
If the same product appears on the waste log repeatedly, that product needs review.
If the same station creates waste repeatedly, that station needs training or process correction.
If the same manager keeps approving overproduction, manager accountability is needed.
If product expires often, ordering and rotation need review.
If remakes are frequent, recipes, order entry, kitchen accuracy, or guest communication may need review.
Waste is information.
The restaurant should use that information to make better decisions.
WHY WASTE MUST BE TRACKED
Waste must be tracked because untracked waste becomes invisible.
Invisible waste cannot be corrected.
A restaurant may believe waste is low because no one is talking about it.
But if food is being thrown away without documentation, managers may not see the real cost.
Employees may throw away product during prep.
Cooks may remake food without writing it down.
Servers may return food to the kitchen without documentation.


Managers may approve comps without connecting them to kitchen waste.
Expired product may be discarded before inventory.
Burned product may go straight to the trash.
Dropped product may never be reported.
All of that waste matters.
The restaurant paid for that product.
When waste is tracked, managers can begin to see patterns.
The pattern matters more than a single mistake.
One mistake may be normal.
Repeated mistakes show a system problem.
For example:
If lettuce is wasted every week, ordering or prep may be too high.
If steaks are repeatedly overcooked, training or cooking standards need review.
If sauces expire, batch sizes may be too large.
If fries are thrown away after every rush, production timing may need review.
If desserts are slow-moving, ordering or menu placement may need review.
If the same item is remade often, order accuracy or recipe execution may be weak.
Waste tracking also teaches employees that product matters.
When employees know waste must be written down, they become more aware.
They may ask before making extra prep.
They may portion more carefully.
They may label product correctly.
They may rotate older product first.
They may tell a manager when something is close to expiration.
They may take fewer shortcuts.
A waste log is not meant to shame employees.
It is meant to create awareness.
Awareness creates accountability.
Accountability reduces loss.
Managers should keep waste tracking simple enough to use during real shifts.
If the waste log is too complicated, employees will not use it.
A good waste log should capture the most important details quickly:
Date
Product
Amount
Reason
Station
Employee or manager initials
Corrective action
The goal is not perfect paperwork.
The goal is better visibility.
Waste that is written down can be reviewed.
Waste that is reviewed can be reduced.

PREP WASTE REVIEW
Prep waste happens before the guest ever orders.
That is why it must be controlled early.
Prep waste can come from overproduction, poor forecasting, wrong batch size, poor knife skills, incorrect recipes, expired
product, trim loss, labeling mistakes, storage problems, or unclear par levels.
A prep employee may work hard and still create waste if the prep plan is wrong.
That is why managers must review prep waste as a system issue, not only an employee issue.
Common prep waste problems include:
Making too much product
Making product too early
Making the wrong product
Using the wrong recipe
Cutting product incorrectly
Trimming too much usable product
Mixing old and new product
Failing to label or date product
Using product out of rotation
Opening backup product too soon
Not checking what is already prepped
Not communicating low or excess product
Prep waste can be expensive because it often involves labor and product.
The restaurant pays for the product.
The restaurant pays the employee to prep it.
Then the restaurant loses both if the product is not used correctly.
Prep waste also affects storage.
Too much prep can crowd the walk-in, hide older product, make rotation harder, and increase spoilage risk.
Too little prep can cause rush problems, delays, menu outages, and emergency production during service.
The goal is not simply to prep less.
The goal is to prep smarter.
Managers should compare prep to actual sales and usage.
If a product is consistently leftover at the end of the day, the prep amount may be too high.
If a product runs out during peak times, the prep amount may be too low.
If a product expires before use, batch size or par level may need adjustment.

If trim waste is high, knife skills or product quality may need review.
If recipes are inconsistent, prep training may need review.
Prep waste review should happen regularly because prep decisions affect the whole restaurant.
Strong prep control protects food cost, ticket times, quality, and service.

COOK LINE WASTE REVIEW
Cook line waste happens during service.
It often happens under pressure.
A cook may burn food, overcook protein, make the wrong item, miss a modifier, drop product, use the wrong portion, hold
product too long, or prepare food that never gets sold.
Cook line waste can be caused by employee mistakes, unclear recipes, poor communication, ticket confusion, weak expo
control, poor prep setup, bad equipment, rushing, or manager decisions.
Cook line waste should be reviewed carefully because it directly affects food cost and guest experience.
When food is wasted on the line, the restaurant may lose more than the product.
It may also lose time, labor, guest trust, and ticket flow.
For example:
A steak cooked to the wrong temperature may need to be remade.
A wrong modifier may delay a table.
A burned item may slow the kitchen.
A missing side may create guest frustration.
A dropped plate may require a full replacement.
A line cook using too much product may increase cost every shift.
A product held too long may become unusable.
Cook line waste often reveals training gaps.
The employee may not understand the recipe.
The ticket may be hard to read.
The station may be poorly set up.
The cook may not know the correct portion tool.
The expo may not be checking plates before they leave.
The manager may not be watching repeated mistakes.
Managers should review cook line waste by product, station, shift, and cause.
The goal is not to punish every mistake.
The goal is to identify patterns and correct them.
One burned item may be a normal mistake.
The same burned item several times in one week needs review.
One wrong modifier may be a communication issue.
Repeated modifier mistakes need POS, server, ticket, or kitchen review.
Cook line waste is a sign that a process needs attention.
Managers should use the waste review to protect both food cost and service quality.

RETURNED FOOD AND REMAKE TRACKER
Returned food and remakes should be tracked because they can quietly damage food cost.
A returned plate is not only a guest service issue.
It is also a product loss issue.
A remake is not only a kitchen correction.
It is also a food cost event.
When food is returned, remade, discounted, voided, or comped, the restaurant should understand why.
Sometimes the restaurant made a mistake.
Sometimes the guest changed their mind.


Sometimes the server entered the wrong item.
Sometimes the kitchen missed a modifier.
Sometimes the food was cooked incorrectly.
Sometimes the plate took too long.
Sometimes the item did not match the guest’s expectation.
Sometimes the food was correct, but the guest was unhappy.
The reason matters.
If managers do not track returned food and remakes, they may miss repeated problems.
For example:
A steak temperature may be missed several times in one week.
A salad may be made without a modifier repeatedly.
A server may enter orders incorrectly.
A kitchen station may be rushing and missing details.
A menu description may be confusing guests.
An item may be commonly returned because expectations are unclear.
A recipe may not be consistent.
A product quality issue may be creating complaints.
Returned food and remakes connect food cost, service, training, and guest experience.
That is why they should be reviewed by managers.
The goal is not to blame employees automatically.
The goal is to find the cause.
If the cause is server order entry, retrain POS use and order confirmation.
If the cause is kitchen execution, retrain recipe and ticket reading.
If the cause is guest expectation, review menu wording or server communication.
If the cause is product quality, review supplier, storage, prep, or cooking method.
If the cause is ticket time, review kitchen flow or staffing.
If the cause is repeated by the same person, coach and follow up.
If the cause is repeated by the same station, review the station system.
Returned food and remakes should be handled quickly with the guest.
But after the guest is taken care of, the manager should document what happened.
A problem that is documented can be corrected.
A problem that is ignored usually repeats.

PART 5
PORTION CONTROL SYSTEM
Purpose of This Section
Portion control is one of the most important parts of food cost control.
A restaurant can count inventory, build par levels, track waste, and order carefully, but still lose money if portions are
inconsistent.
Portion control protects the recipe.
It protects food cost.
It protects guest consistency.
It protects plate presentation.
It protects inventory accuracy.
It protects the restaurant from silent loss.
Over-portioning is easy to miss because it may not look like waste.
The product is not thrown away.
The product is sold.
But if the restaurant gives away more product than the recipe allows, food cost increases.
A little extra on one plate may not seem serious.
A little extra on hundreds of plates becomes expensive.
Under-portioning creates a different problem.
If employees give guests less than the recipe standard, guests may feel cheated.
The plate may look weak.
Complaints may increase.
The restaurant may protect cost in the wrong way and damage trust.
The goal of portion control is not to make plates small.
The goal is to make plates correct.
Correct portions help the restaurant serve the same item the same way every time.
Guests should not receive a large portion one visit and a smaller portion the next visit.
Managers should not allow portions to depend on which cook is working.
Employees should not guess at high-cost ingredients.
Portion control should be trained, measured, observed, corrected, and followed up.
A strong portion control system includes:
Recipe standards
Portion tools
Scoops
Ladles
Scales
Measuring cups
Portion cups
Spoodles
Cut sizes
Plate diagrams if available
Trainer demonstration
Employee practice
Manager observation
Portion audits
Coaching when standards slip
Portion control affects many areas of the restaurant.
Cook line portions affect plate cost.
Prep portions affect recipe yield.
Bar portions affect alcohol cost.
Dessert portions affect consistency.
Sauces and dressings affect both cost and guest experience.
High-cost ingredients need the tightest control.
Managers should pay special attention to proteins, seafood, cheese, specialty toppings, sauces, dressings, alcohol,
premium sides, and any product that is expensive or wasted often.
A restaurant should never rely only on “eyeballing” important portions.
If a product matters to cost, it needs a standard.
If it needs a standard, employees need tools.
If employees need tools, managers need to verify that the tools are being used.
That is the purpose of this section.

WHY PORTION CONTROL PROTECTS PROFIT
Portion control protects profit because it keeps the recipe connected to the cost.
A recipe is built with an expected amount of product.
The menu price is usually based on that expected amount.
When employees use more than the recipe allows, the restaurant gives away product that was not priced into the plate.
That extra product may seem small.
But small portion mistakes repeat quickly in restaurants.
A little extra cheese on every plate.
A larger scoop of dressing.
A heavy handful of fries.
A bigger portion of protein.
Too much sauce.
Extra garnish.
An oversized dessert slice.
An over-poured drink.
A few extra ounces of product can quietly raise food cost every day.

PORTION TOOL CHECKLIST
Portion control is difficult without the right tools.
Employees should not be expected to guess portions during a busy shift.
A restaurant that wants consistent portions must provide the tools needed to measure those portions correctly.
Portion tools protect the recipe, the employee, the guest, and the restaurant.
Without portion tools, employees often rely on habit, memory, speed, or visual judgment.
That creates inconsistency.
One cook may use a heavier scoop.
Another cook may use a smaller scoop.
One bartender may pour carefully.
Another may over-pour.
One prep employee may fill containers accurately.
Another may guess.
One server may portion sauces correctly.
Another may give away extra without realizing the cost.
Portion tools make the standard easier to follow.
Common portion tools include:
Scales
Scoops
Ladles
Spoodles
Measuring cups
Measuring spoons
Portion cups
Cut guides
Bottle pourers
Jiggers
Tongs
Preset containers
Recipe cards
Plate diagrams
Prep yield sheets
The tool should match the product.
A scale may be best for proteins, cheese, dough, seafood, or high-cost ingredients.
A ladle may be best for soups, sauces, gravies, dressings, or batters.
A scoop may be best for sides, salads, desserts, rice, mashed potatoes, or prepared mixtures.
A portion cup may be best for sauces, dressings, condiments, toppings, and sides.
A jigger or measured pour tool may be needed for alcohol control.
A cut guide may be needed for desserts, proteins, breads, or garnishes.
A portion tool is only useful if employees use it.
Managers should check that tools are available, clean, labeled if needed, stored in the correct station, and used during
real service.
If the correct tool is missing, employees will guess.
If the tool is dirty, employees may skip it.
If the tool is stored far away, employees may not use it during a rush.
If multiple similar tools are available, employees may use the wrong one.
If a recipe says “one scoop” but does not identify the scoop size, the standard is incomplete.

A strong portion tool system should answer:
What tool is required?
Where is the tool stored?
What size is the tool?
What product uses the tool?
Who checks that the tool is available?
What happens if the tool is missing?
How often is the tool inspected?
Portion tools should be part of station setup.
If a station cannot open without the correct product, it should not open without the correct portion tools.
Managers should treat portion tools as part of the food cost control system.

RECIPE PORTION STANDARD PAGE
A recipe portion standard explains exactly how much product should be used for a menu item, prep item, side item, sauce,
drink, dessert, or station task.
Without a recipe portion standard, employees may guess.
Guessing creates inconsistent food cost and inconsistent guest experience.
A recipe should not only list ingredients.
It should also explain the amount, tool, method, yield, and finished standard.
For example, a recipe should not say:
Add cheese.
It should say:
Add ______ ounces of cheese using the assigned scale or portion cup.
A recipe should not say:
Use sauce.

It should say:
Use ______ ounces of sauce using the ______ ladle.
A recipe should not say:
Serve fries.
It should say:
Serve ______ ounces of fries using the assigned basket, scoop, or portion standard.
Clear portion standards help employees perform the task correctly.
They also help managers coach employees fairly.
A manager cannot correct an employee clearly if the standard is vague.
A strong recipe portion standard should include:
Menu item name
Ingredient list
Exact portion amount
Unit of measure
Required portion tool
Prep yield
Serving size
Plate or container standard
Garnish standard
Common mistakes
High-cost ingredients
Allergen or food safety concerns
Manager verification steps
Recipe portion standards should be especially clear for items that affect food cost.
High-cost proteins, seafood, cheese, specialty toppings, alcohol, sauces, dressings, desserts, premium sides, and limited-
time specials should never be left to guesswork.
Managers should also watch items that are frequently modified by guests.
If a menu item is often changed, employees need to know how modifications affect portioning, cost, and guest
expectation.
Recipe portion standards should be reviewed during training.
The trainer should explain the recipe.
The trainer should demonstrate the portion.
The employee should practice the portion.
The manager or trainer should observe the employee during real service.
The employee should not be signed off until they can repeat the portion correctly.
A written recipe standard protects the restaurant from silent food cost loss.
It also protects employees from unclear expectations.

OVER-PORTIONING REVIEW
Over-portioning happens when employees use more product than the recipe allows.
It may look like good service.
It may feel generous.
It may even make a guest happy in the moment.
But over-portioning creates long-term problems for the restaurant.
It raises food cost.
It makes recipe costing inaccurate.
It makes inventory usage harder to understand.
It creates inconsistent guest expectations.
It teaches employees that the written standard does not matter.
It can make the correct portion look wrong later.
Over-portioning is often unintentional.
Employees may not know the correct portion.
The portion tool may be missing.

The recipe may be unclear.
The employee may be rushing.
The employee may believe larger portions prevent complaints.
The employee may copy another employee who portions incorrectly.
The manager may not be watching.
The station may not have a visual plate guide.
Over-portioning can happen in many areas:
Proteins
Seafood
Cheese
Pasta
Fries
Sides
Sauces
Dressings
Desserts
Toppings
Garnishes
Alcohol
Bar mixers
To-go condiments
The most dangerous over-portioning happens with high-cost products or high-volume items.
A high-cost item loses money quickly.
A high-volume item loses money repeatedly.
A product that is both high-cost and high-volume requires serious manager attention.
Managers should correct over-portioning quickly and professionally.
The correction should focus on the standard.
A manager can say:
“This portion needs to match the recipe.”
“Use the scale every time.”
“This scoop is the required tool.”
“This ingredient is high cost, so we have to control it carefully.”
“The guest should get the correct portion every visit.”
“Consistency is the standard.”
The goal is not to embarrass employees.
The goal is to train accuracy.
Managers should document repeated over-portioning and follow up with retraining.
If the same employee continues over-portioning after training, the issue becomes accountability.
If the whole station is over-portioning, the issue is likely training, tools, recipe clarity, or manager follow-up.
Over-portioning should never be ignored because it is “only a little extra.”
A little extra becomes a lot when it happens all week.

A guest may feel the value is not fair.
A guest may complain.
A guest may not return.
A server may lose trust with the kitchen.
A manager may need to comp, remake, discount, or apologize.
The restaurant may save a small amount of product and lose a much larger amount in guest satisfaction.

Food cost control should never mean shorting the guest.
The correct portion is the standard.
Not more.
Not less.
Correct.
Under-portioning can happen for several reasons.
An employee may not know the portion standard.
A portion tool may be missing.
A cook may be trying to stretch product because the station is low.
A prep employee may have produced too little.
A manager may have ordered too little.
A cook may be rushing.
A server may request extra after seeing a weak plate.
A guest may compare the plate to a previous visit.
Under-portioning can be just as damaging as over-portioning because it creates inconsistency.
If a guest receives a strong portion one visit and a weak portion the next visit, the guest may lose confidence in the
restaurant.
Consistency is part of value.
A restaurant should protect food cost by reducing waste, improving ordering, controlling prep, following recipes, and using
correct portion tools.
It should not protect food cost by serving less than the standard.

Was the recipe standard clear?
Was the correct portion tool available?
Was the employee trained?
Was the product low?

If under-portioning is happening because employees are guessing, the manager should retrain portion standards.
If under-portioning is happening because the recipe standard is unclear, the manager should update the recipe.
If under-portioning is happening because employees are trying to reduce food cost the wrong way, the manager should

The goal is a consistent plate every time.

PORTION TRAINING SIGN-OFF
Portion control must be trained before it can be enforced.
A manager should not hold an employee accountable for a portion standard that was never explained, demonstrated,
practiced, and verified.
A portion training sign-off gives the restaurant documentation that the employee was trained on the correct standard.
It also helps the employee understand that portion control matters.
Training should include more than telling the employee the portion.
The trainer should explain why the portion matters.
The trainer should show the correct tool.
The trainer should demonstrate the portion.
The employee should practice the portion.
The trainer should correct mistakes.
The employee should repeat the portion correctly.
The manager or trainer should observe the portion during real shift conditions.
Only then should the employee be signed off.
Portion training should focus especially on high-cost items, high-volume items, and items that are frequently made
incorrectly.
Examples include:
Steaks
Seafood
Chicken
Cheese
Pizza toppings
Pasta portions
Fries
Sauces
Dressings
Desserts
Bar pours
Premium sides
Salad toppings
To-go sauces
Specialty ingredients
A portion training sign-off protects the employee and the restaurant.
It helps the employee know exactly what is expected.
It helps the manager coach fairly.
It helps the restaurant prove that the standard was trained.
It also helps identify who needs retraining when portion problems repeat.
Managers should not treat sign-off as a formality.
A signature should mean the employee can perform the portion correctly.
If the employee is not ready, the manager should write down what needs more practice and schedule follow-up.
Training is not complete until the employee can do the task correctly during real restaurant conditions.
Portion control should be reviewed again if the recipe changes, the tool changes, the menu item changes, the employee
transfers stations, or portion mistakes begin repeating.
A strong portion training system reduces guessing.
Less guessing means better consistency.

PORTION AUDIT FORM
A portion audit helps managers verify that employees are following portion standards during real restaurant conditions.
Training is important.
Sign-off is important.
But managers still need to observe the standard during service.

A portion can look correct during training and drift during busy shifts.
Employees may start guessing.
Tools may go missing.
Recipes may be ignored.
High-cost ingredients may be used too heavily.
Portions may change depending on who is working.
A portion audit gives managers a way to check the standard before food cost problems grow.
A portion audit should be simple and practical.
The manager should choose a product, menu item, station, or employee to observe.
The manager should compare the actual portion to the written standard.
The manager should confirm that the correct portion tool is being used.
The manager should document whether the portion is accurate, too heavy, too light, inconsistent, or unclear.
The manager should correct mistakes immediately when needed.
Portion audits are especially useful for:
High-cost ingredients
High-volume menu items
New employees
Employees with repeated portion issues
Stations with high waste
Menu items with inconsistent presentation
Bar pours
Dessert portions
Sauces and dressings
Pizza or salad toppings
Protein portions
A portion audit should not be used only when food cost is bad.
It should be part of the manager’s routine.
When employees know portion standards are reviewed regularly, they are more likely to follow them.
The tone matters.
Managers should present portion audits as standard protection, not punishment.
A manager can say:
“We are checking portions today to protect consistency.”
“We need every plate to match the recipe.”
“This tool must be used every time.”
“This portion is too heavy and needs correction.”
“This portion is too light and could affect guest value.”
“Let’s reset the standard now.”
The best portion audits lead to immediate improvement.
If the issue is small, coach in the moment.
If the issue repeats, document retraining.
If the standard is unclear, fix the recipe.
If tools are missing, replace them.
If the station is repeatedly off-standard, assign manager follow-up.
Portion audits protect profit without lowering quality.
They help the restaurant serve the right portion every time.

Purpose of This Section
Prep planning affects food cost before the shift begins.
When prep is planned well, the restaurant has enough product to serve guests without creating unnecessary waste.
When prep is planned poorly, the restaurant either runs out or throws product away.
Both problems hurt the business.
Under-prepping creates stress during service.
The kitchen may fall behind.
Employees may rush.
Ticket times may increase.
Guests may wait longer.
Menu items may become unavailable.
Managers may need emergency prep during peak hours.
Over-prepping creates waste.
Product may expire.
Storage may become crowded.
Older product may get hidden.
Employees may use newer product before older product.
Managers may order less accurately because too much prepared product is sitting in the walk-in.
Prep planning should be based on facts.
A prep list should not be created only from habit.
Managers and prep leads should consider:
Current inventory
Current prepared product
Sales forecast
Par levels
Upcoming reservations
Events
Weather
Holidays
Product shelf life
Waste patterns
Previous shortages
Previous overproduction
Staffing levels
Station needs
Menu specials
Prep planning is not just a kitchen task.
It is a manager control system.
A strong prep plan helps the restaurant know:
What needs to be prepped
How much should be prepped
Who is responsible
When it should be completed
What product should be used first
What batch size should be made
What should not be prepped yet
What should be watched during the shift
What needs manager approval
Prep production should be tracked because it connects directly to waste.
If a product is made but not sold, the manager should know why.
If a product runs out, the manager should know why.
If a product is prepped every day but often thrown away, the par level or batch size may need adjustment.
If a product is always low, prep planning may need adjustment.
Prep planning protects the shift.
Production control protects the profit.
The purpose of this section is to help managers build a clear prep system that supports both service and food cost control.

DAILY PREP PLAN
A daily prep plan turns the prep list into a shift plan.
The prep list shows what needs to be made.
The daily prep plan helps managers decide when it should be made, who should make it, what should be made first, and
what must be verified before service.
Prep planning should happen before the restaurant is under pressure.
During a rush, it is harder to make good decisions.
Employees may prep too much because they are nervous.
Employees may prep too little because they are rushed.
Employees may skip labels, dates, cooling steps, recipe standards, or storage steps.
Managers may miss problems because service has already started.
A daily prep plan helps prevent that.
A strong daily prep plan should include:
Expected sales
Expected guest volume
Reservations or events
Products already prepped
Products close to expiration
Products that need to be used first
Products that should not be prepped yet
Prep priorities
Assigned employees
Completion deadlines
Manager verification
Follow-up notes
The plan should also separate high-priority prep from low-priority prep.
Not every prep item has the same urgency.
Some items must be ready before service begins.
Some items can wait until later.
Some items should only be made if sales volume increases.
Some items should be held until a manager approves them.
This matters because unnecessary prep creates waste.
The daily prep plan should also protect products with short shelf life.
If an item spoils quickly, managers should avoid making too much at once.
If an item is expensive, managers should review the amount before production begins.
If an item was wasted yesterday, managers should adjust the plan today.
A daily prep plan should not be created once and ignored.
Managers should check progress during the day.
If sales are higher than expected, prep may need to increase.
If sales are lower than expected, prep may need to slow down.
If a product is running low, the manager should decide whether to prep more or adjust ordering.
If a product is sitting unused, the manager should reduce production or push usage before expiration.
Good prep planning is active.
It changes based on the shift.
The goal is to have enough product ready without creating avoidable waste.

PREP PAR LEVEL REVIEW
Prep par levels help managers decide how much prepared product should normally be available.
A prep par is different from an inventory par.
Inventory par focuses on raw product or purchased product.
Prep par focuses on finished or partially prepared product.
For example, a restaurant may have inventory pars for cases of lettuce, tomatoes, cheese, sauce ingredients, chicken, or
dough.
The prep par may be the number of pans, containers, portions, bags, bottles, or batches that should be ready for service.
Prep par levels are important because they help managers balance readiness and waste.
If prep par levels are too high, product may expire before use.
If prep par levels are too low, the restaurant may run out during service.
Prep par levels should be based on real sales and usage.
A manager should not set prep pars only by habit.
The restaurant should review:
Average daily usage
Peak day usage
Slow day usage
Shelf life
Batch size
Storage space
Prep time
Sales forecast
Waste history
Shortage history
Menu popularity
Station needs
Prep par levels should change when business changes.
A Friday night prep par may be different from a Tuesday lunch prep par.
A summer patio season prep par may be different from a slow winter prep par.
A holiday weekend prep par may need temporary adjustment.
A special menu item may need its own prep par.
A product with frequent waste should have its prep par reviewed.
A product that runs out often should also have its prep par reviewed.

The goal is not to keep the walk-in full of prepared product.
The goal is to keep the restaurant ready with the right amount of product.
A good prep par tells employees:
How much should be ready before service.
How much backup should exist.
When to make more.
When to stop.
When to ask a manager.
Prep pars should be written clearly.
If the prep par says “enough,” the standard is weak.
If the prep par says “two full pans,” “one half batch,” “six quart containers,” or “twenty-four portions,” the standard is
clearer.
Prep par levels should be reviewed weekly until they are accurate.
If waste is high, reduce or adjust.
If shortages repeat, increase or adjust.
If sales change, review again.
Prep par levels are not perfect numbers.
They are working targets that help managers control production.

PRODUCTION AND USAGE TRACKER
A production and usage tracker helps managers compare what was made to what was actually used.
This is one of the best ways to find overproduction, underproduction, waste, and prep planning mistakes.
A restaurant may believe its prep levels are correct, but the usage tracker may show something different.
If the restaurant makes four pans of a product and only uses one, the prep level may be too high.
If the restaurant makes two pans and runs out every Friday, the prep level may be too low.
If the restaurant makes product daily but throws away leftovers twice a week, the batch size may be wrong.
If the restaurant makes product in the morning and runs out by dinner, production timing may need to change.
Production tracking helps managers make better decisions because it connects prep to actual use.
It answers:
What did we make?
How much did we make?
When did we make it?

Who made it?
How much did we use?
How much was left?
How much was wasted?
Did we run out?

Did we make too little?

Start with the items causing the biggest problems.

Production and usage tracking helps turn opinions into facts.
Instead of saying “we always make too much,” the manager can show the amount made and amount left.
Instead of saying “we keep running out,” the manager can show which days and volumes caused the shortage.
Facts help the team improve.
A strong production system reduces waste and protects service.

OVERPRODUCTION REVIEW
Overproduction happens when the restaurant makes more product than it can use.
It is one of the most common causes of food waste.
Overproduction can happen in prep, on the cook line, in the bar, during buffet or event service, with sauces, sides,
desserts, proteins, produce, soups, garnishes, or any prepared item.
Overproduction often happens because employees want to be prepared.
That intention is understandable.
But preparation without control creates waste.
A restaurant should be ready for service, not overloaded with product.
Overproduction can be caused by:
Prep pars set too high
Sales forecast too high
Batch size too large
Manager not checking existing product
Employee making product from habit
Fear of running out
Poor communication between shifts
Not using older product first
Product made too early
Slow sales
Menu changes
Weather changes
Large party cancellation
Supplier packaging size
Lack of manager approval
Overproduction creates several problems.
Product may expire before it is used.
Storage areas become crowded.
FIFO becomes harder.
Employees may open new product instead of using older product.
Managers may undercount or overcount inventory.
Food cost increases.
Labor is wasted because employees spent time making product the restaurant did not need.
Overproduction should be reviewed without automatically blaming the prep employee.
The employee may have followed the prep list.
The prep list may have been wrong.
The manager may have failed to check product on hand.
The par level may be outdated.
The batch size may not match current sales.
The forecast may have been wrong.
The purpose of an overproduction review is to find the cause and correct it.


Managers should ask:
Was the product already available?
Was the prep amount based on real need?
Was the sales forecast accurate?
Was the batch size too large?
Was the par level too high?
Did the employee ask before making extra?
Did the manager verify the prep plan?
Did the product have a short shelf life?
Could the product have been used in another way?
What should change next time?
Overproduction is preventable when managers review before product is made.

UNDERPRODUCTION REVIEW
Underproduction happens when the restaurant does not make enough prepared product to support the shift.
Underproduction can damage guest experience, ticket times, employee confidence, and manager control.
When the restaurant runs out of prepared product during service, employees may have to rush.
Rushed prep can lead to mistakes.
Cooks may fall behind.
Servers may wait longer for food.
Guests may experience slower service.
Managers may need to make decisions under pressure.
Menu items may become unavailable.
Underproduction is not always caused by laziness or poor employee effort.
It may come from a weak prep plan.
It may come from low par levels.

PREP COMMUNICATION LOG
Prep communication affects the entire restaurant.
The prep team, cook line, managers, servers, bar, and dish area all depend on accurate communication.
When prep communication is weak, problems spread quickly.
A product may be low, but no one tells the manager.
A prep item may be finished, but the cook line does not know where it is stored.
A product may be close to expiration, but no one identifies it as use-first.
A prep employee may need more product, but the order has already been placed.
A cook may assume backup product exists when it does not.
A manager may think prep is complete when several items are unfinished.
A closing manager may not tell the opening manager what needs attention.
Prep communication is not just talking.
It is a system.
A strong prep communication system tells the team:
What was completed
What was not completed
What is running low
What product must be used first
What product should not be prepped again
What product has quality concerns
What product needs ordering
What product needs manager approval
What product needs follow-up tomorrow
What station needs support
Prep communication should happen before service, during service, and after service.
Before service, managers should confirm readiness.
During service, employees should communicate shortages, waste, quality issues, and production needs.
After service, managers should review what was overproduced, what ran out, and what should change tomorrow.
The prep communication log helps managers prevent repeated mistakes.
If the same issue appears often, the restaurant can fix the system.
For example:
If the line keeps running out of sauce, prep par may be too low.
If the prep team keeps making product already available, storage visibility may be weak.
If product is repeatedly close to expiration, ordering or batch size may need review.
If managers are surprised by shortages, communication between shifts may be weak.
If cooks do not know what needs to be used first, labeling and handoff need improvement.
Good prep communication reduces waste and protects service.

A restaurant should not depend on memory alone.
Important prep information should be written down.

PART 7
STORAGE, ROTATION, AND LABELING
Purpose of This Section
Storage, rotation, and labeling protect food cost before product is ever cooked or served.
A restaurant can order the right product, prep the right amount, and train correct portions, but still lose money if product is
stored poorly.
Poor storage creates waste.
Poor rotation creates expired product.
Poor labeling creates confusion.
Poor dating creates food safety risk.
Poor organization creates inaccurate inventory.
Poor visibility creates over-ordering.
Poor follow-up creates repeated loss.
Storage is not just where product sits.
Storage is part of the food cost control system.
A strong storage system helps employees know where product belongs, what should be used first, what is ready for
service, what should not be used, what needs manager review, and what must be reordered.
A weak storage system forces employees to guess.
They may open new product before older product.
They may miss product during inventory.
They may leave product uncovered.
They may put raw product in the wrong place.
They may forget when something was prepped.
They may throw away product that could have been used earlier.
They may serve product that should have been reviewed by a manager.
This section helps managers review the most important storage control areas:
Storage standards
Walk-in cooler organization
Walk-in freezer organization
Dry storage organization
FIFO rotation
Labeling and dating
Product quality
Storage problem correction
The goal is simple.
Every product should have a place.
Every open product should have a clear label when needed.
Every prepared product should have a date.
Older product should be used first.
Questionable product should be reviewed.
Expired product should not be counted as usable inventory.
Managers should inspect storage areas regularly.
Employees should be trained on storage expectations.
Food cost control depends on product being protected after it enters the building.
Strong storage protects quality, safety, inventory accuracy, and profit.

FIFO ROTATION CHECKLIST
FIFO stands for First In, First Out.
It means older product should be used before newer product.
FIFO is one of the simplest and most important food cost control habits in a restaurant.
When FIFO is ignored, product can expire while newer product is used first.
That creates waste.
It also creates food safety and quality problems.
FIFO should apply to many areas of the restaurant:
Walk-in cooler
Walk-in freezer
Dry storage
Prep area
Cook line backup
Bar storage
Sauces
Dressings
Produce
Proteins
Dairy
Desserts
Bread products
Prepared items
To-go supplies if applicable
FIFO is not only a storage rule.
It is a daily behavior.
Employees must rotate product when deliveries arrive.
Prep employees must use older product first.
Cooks must open older product before new product.
Managers must check that older product is visible.
Receivers must put away deliveries correctly.
Closing employees must leave product organized for the next shift.
FIFO fails when employees are rushed, storage is crowded, labels are missing, dates are unclear, or managers do not
inspect.
A product can be labeled and still not rotated.
A product can be dated and still be hidden.
A product can be in the building and still be wasted because no one saw it.
Managers should teach employees that FIFO protects food cost and guest quality.
Using older product first does not mean using bad product.
It means using safe, usable product in the correct order before it loses quality.

Questionable product should always be reviewed by a manager.
FIFO should be checked every day in high-risk areas and weekly during manager reviews.
If a product repeatedly expires, the manager should check whether FIFO is being followed.
If a product is found behind newer product, the manager should correct the storage habit immediately.
A restaurant that follows FIFO consistently reduces avoidable waste.

Cooks may struggle to prepare the item correctly.
Guests may complain.

Using the product quickly if still acceptable

Rejecting product during receiving
Requesting supplier credit
Adjusting order quantity

Following up with supplier

A container is missing a label.
A product is stored in the wrong place.

A product is uncovered.
A shelf is crowded.
A delivery is put away incorrectly.

A box is damaged.
A prepared product is not dated.
A freezer item is buried.


A dry storage product is close to expiration.
Each problem matters because storage problems create food cost risk.

FOOD COST PROBLEM FINDER
A food cost problem finder helps managers identify where food cost loss is actually happening.
Food cost problems are not always obvious.
A manager may see that food cost is high, but the number alone does not explain the cause.
Food cost can rise because of many different issues.
Inventory may be inaccurate.
Orders may be too large.
Product may be wasted.
Portions may be too heavy.
Prep may be overproduced.
Product may expire.
Supplier prices may increase.
Deliveries may be short or damaged.
Recipes may not be followed.
Product may be used without being rung in correctly.
Managers may miss follow-up.
The food cost problem finder helps the manager narrow the issue.
Instead of saying, “Food cost is bad,” the manager can ask:
Where is the loss happening?
What product is involved?
What process failed?
Who needs to correct it?
What action should happen first?
The first step is to identify the symptom.
A symptom is what the manager sees.


Examples include:
Food cost percentage increased
Waste increased
Inventory count does not make sense
Purchases are too high
Product keeps running out
Product keeps expiring
Emergency orders are increasing
A high-cost item is disappearing too fast
Prep leftovers are too high
Guest remakes are increasing
The second step is to identify the likely cause.
The third step is to assign a correction.
Managers should avoid guessing too quickly.
If food cost is high, do not automatically blame waste.
Review inventory, ordering, receiving, prep, portions, storage, and sales mix.
If a product is missing, do not automatically blame theft.
Review waste logs, receiving accuracy, portion control, recipe usage, and count accuracy.
If product expires, do not automatically blame employees.
Review ordering, prep levels, storage visibility, FIFO, sales forecast, and manager inspections.
A strong manager looks for the system problem.
The goal is correction, not accusation.
When managers use a problem finder, they make better decisions because they connect symptoms to causes.

TOP 10 COST RISK ITEMS
Every restaurant has certain items that require more attention than others.
These are cost risk items.
A cost risk item is any product that can hurt food cost quickly if it is not controlled.
Cost risk can come from high price, high volume, short shelf life, frequent waste, poor portion control, slow sales, supplier
issues, or inconsistent recipe use.
Managers should identify the top 10 cost risk items in the restaurant.
These items should receive extra review during inventory, ordering, prep, portion audits, storage checks, waste review,
and manager food cost meetings.
Cost risk items may include:
Steaks
Seafood
Chicken
Ribs
Cheese
Premium toppings
Fresh produce
Sauces
Desserts
Alcohol
Wine
Specialty ingredients
Dough

Bread products
High-volume sides
Expensive garnishes
Limited-time special ingredients
A product does not have to be expensive to be a cost risk.
A low-cost product used in very high volume can still create major loss.
For example, fries, sauce, dressing, bread, to-go containers, or cheese may create large cost issues if portioning or usage
is uncontrolled.
A product may also be a cost risk because it spoils quickly.
Fresh produce, seafood, dairy, prepared sauces, and desserts may become expensive if the restaurant orders or preps
too much.
A product may be a cost risk because employees guess the portion.
A product may be a cost risk because it is used in many recipes and becomes hard to track.
The purpose of the top 10 list is focus.
Managers cannot watch everything equally every day.
But they can choose the most important items and control them carefully.
A top 10 cost risk item should have:
A clear storage location
A clear unit of measure
A clear par level
A clear recipe portion
A required portion tool if needed
A waste tracking process
A manager review schedule
An assigned responsible person
The list should be reviewed monthly or whenever food cost changes.
If a product is no longer a major risk, it can be replaced with another item.
The list should always reflect the restaurant’s current operation.

HIGH-WASTE ITEM REVIEW
A high-waste item is any product that appears on waste logs repeatedly or creates a large cost loss when it is wasted.
High-waste items should receive special manager attention.
Waste does not always come from the most expensive product.
Sometimes waste comes from a product that is used often, prepped often, or handled by many employees.
A high-waste item may be:
Produce
Sauce
Dressing
Bread
Fries
Protein

Seafood
Dessert
Bar garnish
Prepared side
Soup
Dough
Cheese
Rice
Pasta
To-go product
Specialty ingredient
Managers should review high-waste items weekly until the problem improves.
The review should focus on cause.
A product may be wasted because:
Too much was ordered
Too much was prepped
The batch size is too large
The product has a short shelf life
The product is not selling
The product is not rotated
The product is stored poorly
The product is not labeled
Employees are using the wrong portion
Employees are making the wrong recipe
The supplier quality is poor
The product is damaged during receiving
The item is returned or remade often
Managers are not following up
A high-waste item review should not stop at “we wasted too much.”
It should answer why.
For example:
If tomatoes are wasted every week, review ordering, prep cuts, storage, and sales forecast.
If sauce expires, review batch size and prep par.
If bread is thrown away nightly, review daily order amount.
If protein is wasted, review cooking standards and portion control.
If bar fruit is wasted, review garnish prep levels.
If desserts expire, review par levels and menu movement.
The manager should assign one clear correction.
Reducing waste usually works best when the team focuses on a specific action.
Do not simply tell employees, “Waste less.”
Tell them what to do differently.
Examples:
Prep one half batch instead of one full batch.
Use older product first before opening new product.
Reduce the order by one case.
Label and date every container before storage.
Use the scale for every portion.
Review this product at the end of every shift.
Do not prep more without manager approval.
Specific instructions create better results.

MENU ITEM COST CONCERN SHEET
A menu item can create a food cost concern when its ingredients, portion size, waste, prep time, selling price, or sales
volume do not support a healthy profit.
A menu item may be popular and still create cost problems.
A menu item may sell often but use too much expensive product.
A menu item may be priced too low.
A menu item may require too much prep.
A menu item may create too many remakes.

A menu item may have ingredients that spoil before they are used.
A menu item may have inconsistent portions.
A menu item may look profitable but lose money because employees do not follow the recipe.
Managers should review menu items when they see repeated cost concerns.
Examples of warning signs include:
High-cost ingredients
Frequent over-portioning
Frequent remakes
Frequent guest returns
Short shelf-life ingredients
Low sales volume
High prep waste
Complicated prep process
Specialty product used only for one item
Large price increases from supplier
Portion inconsistency
Item selling heavily but food cost rising
Item not selling enough to justify inventory
A menu item cost concern does not always mean the item should be removed.
It may mean the recipe needs to be clarified.
The portion tool may need to be changed.
The menu price may need review.
The prep batch size may need adjustment.
The order amount may need reduction.
The item may need better promotion to use product before it spoils.
The supplier product may need to be changed.
The team may need retraining.
The menu description may need to be clearer.
The manager should review the full item before deciding.
A menu item should be judged by more than popularity.
It should be reviewed by cost, waste, prep demand, consistency, guest satisfaction, and operational difficulty.
A strong menu item supports the restaurant.
A weak menu item may keep selling while quietly hurting profit.
Managers should document concerns so ownership or leadership can make better menu decisions.

MANAGER FOOD COST ACTION PLAN
A food cost review is only useful when it leads to action.
Managers may identify problems, complete forms, review waste, check inventory, and discuss cost concerns, but nothing
improves unless someone follows through.
The manager food cost action plan turns review into correction.
It answers:
What problem are we fixing?
Why is it happening?
What action will be taken?
Who is responsible?
When is it due?
How will we know it improved?
A strong action plan should be specific.
A weak action plan says:
Reduce waste.
Watch portions.
Do better ordering.
Clean the walk-in.

Train employees.
Those statements are too general.
A strong action plan says:
Reduce tomato prep from two full pans to one full pan on Monday through Thursday and review waste after one week.
Audit cheese portions on pizza station during dinner shift for three days and retrain employees who are not using the
scale.
Lower the par level on salmon by one case until sales increase or waste improves.
Reorganize walk-in produce shelf and mark use-first product before opening shift tomorrow.
Review supplier invoice price changes every delivery for the next four orders.
Specific action creates accountability.
The manager should focus on the highest-impact issue first.
Trying to fix every food cost problem at once can overwhelm the team.
Start with the product, station, or process that creates the biggest risk.
Then assign ownership.
A food cost action plan should never end with “everyone.”
Everyone may help, but one person should own the follow-up.
The manager should also set a follow-up date.
Without a follow-up date, the plan may disappear during daily operations.
The action plan should be reviewed again to see whether it worked.
If the problem improved, the restaurant should keep the new standard.
If the problem did not improve, the manager should adjust the plan.
Food cost control is a cycle:
Review the problem.
Find the cause.
Take action.
Follow up.
Adjust if needed.
Repeat.
That cycle is what turns a workbook into a working system.

FOOD COST FOLLOW-UP LOG
Follow-up is where food cost control often succeeds or fails.
Many managers notice problems.
Fewer managers follow up until the problem is corrected.
A follow-up log helps managers track whether assigned actions were completed.
It also helps prevent repeated conversations with no results.
Food cost problems can easily get lost during busy restaurant operations.
A manager may identify waste on Monday.
Then staffing problems happen Tuesday.
A delivery problem happens Wednesday.
A busy weekend starts Friday.
By the next week, the original food cost issue may be forgotten.
The follow-up log keeps the issue visible.
It shows:
What problem was found
What action was assigned
Who was responsible
When it was due
Whether it was completed
Whether the issue improved
What still needs attention
A follow-up log is especially important for repeated problems.
If the same product is wasted every week, follow-up should be documented.
If the same employee keeps missing portion standards, follow-up should be documented.
If the same manager keeps skipping inventory review, follow-up should be documented.
If the same supplier problem repeats, follow-up should be documented.
If the same storage area fails inspection, follow-up should be documented.
Follow-up should be direct and professional.
The goal is not to create blame.
The goal is to make sure the restaurant actually improves.
A manager should ask:
Was the action completed?
Was it completed correctly?
Did the problem improve?
Does more training need to happen?
Does the standard need to change?
Does another manager need to be involved?
Does ownership need to review the issue?
If no one follows up, employees learn that the forms do not matter.
If managers follow up consistently, employees learn that food cost control is part of the operation.
Follow-up builds accountability.
Accountability protects profit.

Where inventory sheets are located
Which storage areas are counted
What order the count should follow
How product categories are organized
What unit of measure should be used
How to count partial cases
How to count open containers
How to handle expired product
How to handle damaged product
How to report missing items from the count sheet
How to flag unusual counts
How to verify high-cost products

The trainer should show how the count sheet matches the storage flow.
The trainer should explain product categories.

Do not ignore damaged product.
Do not assume the count sheet is complete if a product is missing.

PORTION CONTROL COACHING FORM
Portion control coaching helps managers correct portion mistakes professionally and consistently.
Portion mistakes should be corrected quickly because they can affect food cost and guest experience.
But coaching should be fair.
An employee should understand what was wrong, what the correct standard is, why it matters, and what must change
next time.
A portion control coaching form should be used when a portion issue is observed during training, prep, service, audit, or
manager review.
This form may be used for over-portioning, under-portioning, missing portion tools, incorrect recipe execution, inconsistent
plate builds, bar pour issues, dessert portion problems, sauce or dressing issues, or high-cost ingredient concerns.
The purpose of coaching is improvement.
The manager should not simply say, “Do it better.”
The manager should show the employee the correct standard.
The manager should identify the correct portion tool.
The manager should have the employee demonstrate the correct portion.
The manager should explain the reason for the standard.
The manager should document whether follow-up is needed.
Portion coaching should be direct but respectful.
A strong coaching conversation may sound like this:
“This portion is heavier than the recipe allows. We need to use the scale every time so the guest gets the same plate and
the restaurant protects food cost.”
“This portion is too light. That can make the guest feel like the plate is not worth the price. The correct portion is the
standard.”
“This ladle is the wrong size. The recipe uses the smaller ladle. Let’s reset the station now.”
“This item is high cost, so we cannot guess. Use the portion tool every time.”
If the employee was not trained, the solution is training.
If the tool was missing, the solution is tool replacement.
If the recipe was unclear, the solution is recipe clarification.
If the employee was trained and continues to ignore the standard, the issue becomes accountability.
The coaching form helps managers document the correction and follow-up.
It also helps protect consistency across managers.
Portion control should not depend on which manager is working.
The standard should be the same for every shift.

WASTE REDUCTION ACTION PLAN
A waste reduction action plan helps the restaurant move from tracking waste to reducing waste.
Tracking is only the first step.
The restaurant must use the information to make changes.
A waste log shows what was wasted.
A waste pattern review shows what keeps happening.
A waste reduction action plan tells the team what will change next.
Waste reduction should be specific.
A manager should not simply say:
Waste less.
Be careful.
Do better.
That kind of instruction is too vague.
Employees need clear direction.


A stronger instruction sounds like:
Prep one half batch of ranch on slow weekdays instead of one full batch.
Use the oldest lettuce first before opening a new case.
Do not prep more sliced tomatoes after 7:00 p.m. without manager approval.
Log every remake during dinner service this week.
Reduce bread order by one case and review waste after seven days.
Audit steak temperatures during peak dinner shift for three nights.
Clear instructions give the team something to follow.
Waste reduction should focus on the biggest problem first.
Trying to correct every waste issue at once can overwhelm the team.
The manager should choose the item, category, station, or process that creates the most loss or repeats most often.
Waste reduction may require changes in ordering, prep, storage, portioning, recipes, training, or manager follow-up.
The action plan should include:
The waste problem
The product or station involved
The cause
The correction
The person responsible
The deadline
The follow-up date
The measurement of improvement
Waste reduction should be reviewed after the action is taken.
If waste improved, the new standard should become part of the system.
If waste did not improve, the manager should review the cause again.
Sometimes the first correction is not enough.
The manager may need to adjust par levels, change batch sizes, retrain employees, review suppliers, or increase
manager observation.
The goal is progress.
Waste reduction is a habit.
The more consistently managers review and correct waste, the stronger the restaurant becomes.

PREP AND PORTION CONTROL REVIEW
Prep and portion control are closely connected.
Prep determines how much product is made.
Portion control determines how much product is used each time an item is served.
If prep is too high, waste increases.
If prep is too low, service suffers.
If portions are too heavy, food cost rises.
If portions are too light, guest value suffers.
If recipes are unclear, both prep and portion control become inconsistent.
This review helps managers confirm that the restaurant has control over production and recipe execution.
The prep side of the review should answer:
Are prep lists specific?
Are daily prep plans used?
Are prep pars written?
Are batch sizes correct?
Is existing product checked before more is made?
Are products close to expiration used first?
Is overproduction reviewed?
Is underproduction reviewed?
Is prep completion verified?
Is prep communication strong between shifts?
The portion side of the review should answer:
Are recipe portions written?
Are portion tools available?
Are employees trained?
Are high-cost ingredients controlled?
Are over-portioning issues corrected?
Are under-portioning issues corrected?
Are portion audits completed?
Are portion coaching conversations documented?
Prep and portion control should protect both profit and guest experience.
A restaurant should not reduce waste by running out of product.
A restaurant should not reduce food cost by under-serving guests.
A restaurant should not increase guest value by over-portioning without control.
The goal is the right amount of product prepared and the correct amount of product served.
Managers should review prep and portion control together because one area can hide problems in the other.

For example:
If prep keeps running out, portions may be too heavy.
If prep is often leftover, sales forecast or batch size may be wrong.
If food cost is high but waste looks low, over-portioning may be the issue.
If guest complaints increase, under-portioning or inconsistency may be happening.
If inventory usage does not match sales, recipe execution may need review.
Prep and portion control are daily leadership habits.
They need training, tools, observation, and follow-up.

FINAL MANAGER FOOD COST SYSTEM REVIEW
The final manager food cost system review confirms whether the restaurant has a complete working system.
This page should be completed when the manager believes the food cost control process is ready to use.
It may also be completed monthly, quarterly, or after a major food cost issue.
The purpose is to review the full system from start to finish.
Food cost control is not one form.
It is not one meeting.
It is not one inventory count.
It is a combination of connected habits.
The restaurant needs to know:
What product is in the building.
What product is being ordered.
What product is being wasted.
What product is being prepared.
What product is being portioned.
What product is being stored.
What product is being used first.
What product is creating risk.
What employees have been trained.
What managers are following up.
The final review helps leadership see whether the system is strong enough to protect profit.


A complete food cost system should include:
Organized inventory
Clear ordering responsibility
Par levels
Order guides
Receiving checks
Waste tracking
Waste review
Prep planning
Production tracking
Portion standards

FIFO

Employee training
Manager accountability
Action plans
Follow-up logs
If any area is missing, the restaurant can still use the workbook, but the manager should know where the weakness is.
The final review should be honest.
Do not mark an area complete if it is not being used.
A system that looks complete on paper but is not followed in daily work will not protect food cost.
Managers should use this page to decide:
What is ready?
What needs repair?
What needs training?
What needs owner or GM review?

The goal is not perfection.
The goal is a system that managers can use consistently.
