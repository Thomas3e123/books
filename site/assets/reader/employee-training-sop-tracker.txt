Introduction
Restaurant training is one of the biggest differences between a restaurant that runs smoothly and a restaurant that constantly
struggles.
When training is weak, the problems show up everywhere. Employees ask the same questions over and over. Managers repeat
the same corrections every shift. Guests receive inconsistent service. Food quality changes depending on who is working. Side
work gets missed. Opening and closing duties are incomplete. New employees feel overwhelmed. Experienced employees get
frustrated.
Most training problems do not happen because employees do not care. They happen because the restaurant does not have a
clear enough system.
A new employee cannot meet a standard that has never been clearly explained. A trainer cannot train consistently if every trainer
teaches the job differently. A manager cannot hold people accountable if the restaurant has not documented what the correct
process is.
This workbook was created to solve that problem.
The Employee Training & SOP Tracker is designed to help full-service restaurant managers build a practical training system that
can be used during real restaurant operations.
This is not a theory book. This is a working tool.
Inside this workbook, managers will find checklists, trackers, sign-off pages, SOP templates, coaching logs, retraining forms,
review pages, and action plans that help turn training into a repeatable system.
The goal is simple: train employees clearly, track their progress, document what was taught, confirm task mastery, follow up when
standards slip, build stronger SOPs, and create consistency across the restaurant.
A restaurant does not become consistent by hoping employees figure things out. Consistency comes from clear standards,
repeated practice, manager follow-up, and documented accountability.
This workbook gives managers a place to start.


HOW TO USE THIS WORKBOOK
This workbook is built for owners, general managers, assistant managers, shift leaders, department leads, and approved trainers.
Use it as a working system - not as a requirement to complete every page for every employee.
MINIMUM VIABLE EMPLOYEE TRAINING FILE

Onboarding completion record for a new hire.
The correct role tracker plus a Daily Training Shift Log while active training is occurring.
Task-mastery or certification evidence before independent work.
30/60/90-day reviews and coaching/retraining records only when the restaurant's process or an actual issue requires them.

USE THE SMALLEST TOOL THAT SOLVES THE PROBLEM

Do not complete several forms to document the same event. Choose one primary record, assign the next action, set a due date, and follow
up. If a company HR, payroll, safety, or compliance form already controls the same record, use the company form and reference it instead of
duplicating sensitive information here.
OPERATING RHYTHM

Active training: review after each training shift and at least weekly.
Before sign-off: observe performance in real restaurant conditions.
When a standard is missed: diagnose the cause before choosing coaching, retraining, process correction, or accountability.

SIGN-OFF STANDARD

A signature confirms verified readiness - not exposure, attendance, time beside a trainer, or personal confidence. The employee should be
able to perform the task correctly, safely, consistently, and with the level of supervision appropriate to the role.


QUICK-START IMPLEMENTATION GUIDE
Use this guide to select the right workbook tool at the right moment. Do not try to complete the entire workbook at once. Begin
with the employee, position, training stage, or performance issue that needs attention now.
START HERE BEFORE THE EMPLOYEE ARRIVES
□ Complete the New Hire Readiness Checklist.
□ Assign an approved trainer and confirm the training schedule.
□ Select the role-specific tracker for the employee’s position.
□ Gather the correct SOPs, recipes, service standards, and safety procedures.
USE ON THE FIRST DAY
□ Complete the First Day Welcome Checklist and employee information pages.
□ Review appearance, attendance, conduct, safety, sanitation, and service culture.
□ Explain how the employee will be trained, observed, coached, and certified.

□ Record what was explained, demonstrated, practiced, and completed.

□ Observe the employee during real operating conditions.
□ Confirm accuracy, consistency, safety, pace, communication, and recovery skills.
□ Complete the station or position certification page only when the standard is truly met.

Elite standard: A signature confirms verified readiness - not exposure, attendance, or time spent beside a
trainer.


QUICK-START IMPLEMENTATION GUIDE: 90-DAY ROADMAP
BEFORE THE FIRST SHIFT
Prepare the employee file, identify required forms, assign the trainer, confirm the position standard, and schedule the first review
points.
DAY 1
Welcome the employee, explain expectations, complete the restaurant tour, introduce safety and sanitation rules, and begin the
role-specific tracker.
DAYS 2-7
Demonstrate tasks, provide supervised practice, document progress after each shift, identify gaps, and assign the next training
priority.
DAY 7 CHECKPOINT
Review attendance, attitude, task progress, guest interaction, teamwork, safety, and readiness for additional responsibility. Do not
allow unfinished training to become permanent habit.
30-DAY REVIEW
Confirm core task mastery, consistency, reliability, communication, and remaining training needs. Update the employee
development plan.
60-DAY REVIEW
Evaluate performance during real volume, independence, judgment, teamwork, and response to coaching. Schedule retraining
where needed.
90-DAY REVIEW
Make a documented decision: fully approved, approved with continued development, reassigned, retraining required, or not
approved for the position.
WEEKLY MANAGER ROUTINE
□ Review every active trainee and every overdue follow-up.
□ Check for missing signatures, incomplete trackers, and unclear next steps.
□ Confirm trainers are teaching the approved standard consistently.
□ Assign one owner and one due date to every open training action.
MONTHLY SYSTEM ROUTINE
□ Review department training scores, repeated performance issues, certification gaps, and SOPs that need revision.
□ Select the three highest-risk training problems and create a documented correction plan.

PART 1
THE PURPOSE OF RESTAURANT TRAINING


Why Training Breaks Down in Restaurants
Restaurant training usually breaks down for one of five reasons:
1. The standard is not written down.
2. Different people train the same job in different ways.
3. Managers assume employees understood the task.
4. Employees are signed off too quickly.
5. No one follows up after the first few shifts.
In many restaurants, training happens informally.
A new employee follows someone around for a few shifts. They watch, ask questions, and try to remember everything. If the
trainer is strong, the employee may learn well. If the trainer is rushed, distracted, or inconsistent, the employee may leave training
with major gaps.
The problem is not always the trainer.
The problem is the lack of a system.
A strong training system does not leave everything to memory.
It gives the trainer a checklist.
It gives the employee a clear path.
It gives the manager a way to verify progress.
It gives the restaurant documentation.
Training should answer these questions:
What does the employee need to know?
Who is responsible for teaching it?
When should it be taught?
How will the employee practice it?
How will the manager know the employee can do it correctly?
What happens if the employee is not ready?
Without those answers, training becomes guesswork.
Guesswork creates inconsistency.
Inconsistency creates guest complaints, employee frustration, wasted labor, wasted food, poor service, and weak accountability.
A restaurant cannot build strong operations on unclear training.
Clear training protects the guest experience, the employee, the manager, and the business.

The Cost of Poor Training
Poor training is expensive.
It does not always show up as one large cost. Instead, it shows up in small problems that repeat every day.
A server forgets to ring in an add-on.
A cook over-portions an expensive ingredient.
A bartender makes drinks inconsistently.
A host seats the dining room unevenly.
A dishwasher falls behind because the closing process was never clearly explained.
A prep cook makes too much product because par levels were not understood.
A manager spends the shift fixing problems that should have been prevented.
Each problem may seem small by itself.
Together, they cost the restaurant money.
Poor training can increase:
Labor cost
Food cost
Waste
Comps
Voids
Refunds
Turnover
Guest complaints
Manager stress
Employee frustration
Poor training also damages morale.
Good employees do not want to work in chaos. They do not want to constantly cover for people who were never trained properly.
They do not want standards to change depending on who is managing the shift.
When training is unclear, accountability feels unfair.
When training is clear, accountability becomes easier.
A manager can say:
“This is the standard.”
“This is where we trained it.”
“This is where you signed off.”
“This is what needs to improve.”
“This is when we will follow up.”
That kind of accountability is professional.
It is also fair.
The goal of training is not to punish employees.
The goal is to give employees the best possible chance to succeed while protecting the standards of the restaurant.

What Good Training Actually Looks Like
Good restaurant training is clear, organized, hands-on, and documented.
It does not depend on memory alone.
It does not depend on one strong trainer.
It does not change every time a different manager is working.
Good training follows a simple pattern:
Explain the standard.
Show the correct method.
Let the employee practice.
Observe the employee doing the task.
Correct mistakes immediately.
Have the employee repeat the task.
Confirm consistency.
Document completion.
Follow up later.
Training should always move from instruction to demonstration to practice to verification.
An employee should not be signed off just because they heard the explanation.
They should be signed off when they can perform the task correctly during real operating conditions.
For example, a server should not be considered trained only because they were told how to greet a table.
They should be observed greeting a real table.
A cook should not be considered trained only because they were shown a recipe.
They should be observed preparing the item correctly, using the correct portion, plate presentation, timing, and sanitation
standards.
A dishwasher should not be considered trained only because they were told where clean dishes go.
They should be observed handling the dish flow during an actual shift, keeping up with volume, protecting sanitation standards,
and completing closing duties correctly.
Training must be practical because restaurant work is practical.
The employee must know what to do when the restaurant is slow.
They must know what to do when the restaurant is busy.
They must know what to do when something goes wrong.
Good training prepares employees for real situations, not perfect situations.

The Manager’s Role in Training
The manager owns the training system.
A trainer may teach the employee.
An experienced employee may demonstrate the task.
A department lead may help with practice.
But the manager is responsible for making sure the training is clear, complete, documented, and followed up.
Managers should not assume training is complete just because an employee has worked several shifts.
Time worked is not the same as training completed.
An employee can work for weeks and still have serious training gaps if no one verifies task mastery.
The manager’s role is to ask:
Was the employee trained on the correct standard?
Who trained the employee?
Was the task demonstrated?
Did the employee practice?
Was performance observed?
Was the employee corrected?
Was the task signed off?
Does the employee still need follow-up?
Is the employee ready to work without constant supervision?
A manager must protect the standard.
That means training cannot be casual.
It must be intentional.
When managers take training seriously, employees usually take it more seriously too.
A strong manager does not just correct mistakes.
A strong manager builds a system that reduces repeat mistakes.

Training for Consistency, Not Just Completion
The goal of training is not to check boxes.
The goal is consistency.
A completed checklist does not matter if the employee still cannot perform the job correctly.
A signed training page does not matter if the manager never follows up.
A training system only works when it improves the way the restaurant operates.
Consistency means the guest experience does not depend on luck.
It means the guest receives the same standard whether they visit on Monday lunch or Saturday dinner.
It means the food is prepared correctly no matter which cook is working.
It means side work is completed no matter who closes.
It means employees understand expectations before they are held accountable.
Consistency does not happen by accident.
It is built through repeated standards, documented training, clear correction, and manager follow-up.
This workbook should help managers move from verbal training to visible training.
Verbal training is easy to forget.
Visible training can be reviewed, repeated, signed, corrected, and improved.
A restaurant with strong training has fewer surprises.
Employees know what is expected.
Managers know what was taught.
Trainers know what to cover.
Owners can see where the team is improving.
Guests feel the difference.
The purpose of this workbook is to help turn training into a system that lasts.

PART 2
NEW HIRE ONBOARDING


Purpose of This Section:
This section helps managers create a clear, professional onboarding process for every new employee.
Onboarding is more than paperwork.
It is the employee’s first real impression of the restaurant, the management team, the standards, and the culture.
A strong onboarding process helps new employees understand:
- Where they belong
- What is expected
- Who they report to
- What standards matter
- How training will work
- How progress will be measured
- What success looks like in their position
When onboarding is rushed or unclear, employees often start confused. They may not know the schedule, uniform expectations,
service standards, safety rules, station responsibilities, or who to ask for help.
That confusion can turn into mistakes, frustration, poor performance, or early turnover.
A good onboarding process gives the employee direction before they are expected to perform.
This section includes practical checklists and forms that managers can use during the employee’s first day, first week, and early
training period.

PART 3
ROLE-SPECIFIC TRAINING TRACKERS


Purpose of This Section:
Every restaurant position needs clear training.
A server, host, bartender, cook, dishwasher, prep cook, and manager all affect the restaurant in different ways. Each role has
different responsibilities, different risks, different guest impact, and different standards.
Training should not be the same for every position.
A strong restaurant training system gives each employee a clear path based on the job they are actually expected to perform.
This section gives managers position-specific training trackers that can be used during hands-on training, shift observation, follow-
up reviews, and final sign-off.
Each tracker is designed to help managers and trainers answer four important questions:
What has the employee been taught?
What has the employee practiced?
What can the employee perform correctly during real shift conditions?
What still needs follow-up?
Do not sign off on a task just because the employee watched someone else do it.
A task should be signed off only when the employee can complete it correctly, consistently, safely, and without constant
reminders.
The goal is not to rush employees through training.
The goal is to build dependable employees who understand the standard before they are held accountable for the standard.

PART 4
TASK MASTERY SHEETS


WHAT TASK MASTERY MEANS
Purpose of This Section:
Training is not complete when an employee hears instructions.
Training is not complete when an employee watches someone else perform a task.
Training is not complete just because a checklist was reviewed.
Training is complete when the employee can perform the task correctly, safely, consistently, and without constant reminders
during real restaurant conditions.
That is task mastery.
Task mastery means the employee has moved beyond awareness and into dependable performance.
This section gives managers a way to document whether an employee can actually perform key restaurant tasks.
The task mastery sheets can be used for:
Front-of-house duties
Back-of-house duties
Service standards
Cleaning and sanitation
Guest recovery
Cash handling
Opening duties
Closing duties
Station-specific responsibilities
Each task mastery sheet should be completed after training and observation.
The manager or trainer should not sign off unless the employee can demonstrate the task correctly.
If the employee cannot complete the task properly, the manager should assign more practice, coaching, or retraining.
The purpose of this section is to protect consistency.
A restaurant should not depend on memory, assumptions, or verbal promises.
A restaurant should verify that important tasks are being done correctly.

PART 5
SOP BUILDING TEMPLATES


Purpose of This Section:
A restaurant cannot train consistently if the standards only exist in someone’s memory.
Standard Operating Procedures, also called SOPs, give managers and employees a clear written process for important restaurant
tasks.
An SOP explains what should be done, who should do it, when it should happen, what tools or supplies are needed, and what the
correct result should look like.
SOPs are not meant to make the restaurant complicated.
They are meant to make the restaurant clearer.
A strong SOP helps the restaurant:
Train employees faster
Reduce repeated mistakes
Improve consistency
Protect food safety
Improve service standards
Reduce manager correction time
Create fair accountability
Make expectations easier to understand
Keep standards from changing from person to person
An SOP should be simple enough that a new employee can understand it, but complete enough that an experienced employee
cannot claim the standard was unclear.
This section gives managers reusable SOP templates for the most common full-service restaurant needs:
Position tasks
Opening procedures
Closing procedures
Cleaning procedures
Food prep procedures
Service procedures
Safety procedures
SOP review and updates
The goal is not to write a perfect policy manual.
The goal is to document the tasks that affect daily restaurant performance.
Start with the tasks that create the most problems.
If employees keep doing something differently, write an SOP.
If managers keep correcting the same mistake, write an SOP.
If a task affects food safety, guest experience, labor, food cost, cash control, or opening and closing readiness, write an SOP.
A written standard gives the team something to train from, follow, review, correct, and improve.


HOW TO BUILD A SIMPLE RESTAURANT SOP
Purpose:
Use this section to turn repeated restaurant tasks into clear written standards.
A strong SOP should answer:

1. What task is being performed?
2. Who is responsible?
3. When does it happen?
4. Why does it matter?
5. What tools, supplies, forms, or equipment are needed?
6. What steps must be followed?
7. What should the finished result look like?
8. What safety, sanitation, service, or cash control rules apply?
9. Who verifies completion?
10. What should the employee do if something goes wrong?
An SOP does not need to be complicated.
It needs to be clear.
Avoid vague instructions such as:

11. Clean the station properly.

12. Give good service.

13. Prep enough product.

14. Close the bar correctly.

15. Watch food quality.
Those phrases sound helpful, but they do not tell the employee exactly what to do.
A better SOP uses observable standards:

16. Wipe and sanitize all food-contact surfaces.

17. Restock lemons, straws, napkins, beverage napkins, and receipt paper before service.

18. Label and date all prepared product before placing it in the walk-in.

19. Check every plate for correct item, correct side, correct garnish, and clean rim before it leaves the kitchen.

20. Count the drawer according to restaurant policy before closing the shift.
If an employee cannot look at the SOP and understand the expected action, the SOP needs to be clearer.

□ Choose one repeated task.
□ Write the exact task name.
□ Identify the position responsible.


□ Identify when the task should be completed.
□ Watch the task being done correctly.
□ List the steps in the correct order.
□ Add required tools, supplies, equipment, or forms.
□ Add safety or sanitation requirements.
□ Add guest service expectations if applicable.
□ Add food quality or portion standards if applicable.
□ Add cash control or manager approval rules if applicable.
□ Define what the finished result should look like.
□ Identify who verifies completion.
□ Test the SOP with an employee.
□ Revise any unclear steps.
□ Train the team on the SOP.
□ Set a review date.
COMMON SOP MISTAKES
□ SOP is too long.
□ SOP is too vague.
□ SOP is written by someone who does not perform the task.
□ SOP does not include tools or supplies needed.
□ SOP does not explain the finished standard.
□ SOP does not include safety or sanitation.
□ SOP is written but never trained.
□ SOP is trained once but never followed up.
□ SOP is not updated when the process changes.
□ No manager is responsible for verifying the standard.

COMPLETED EXAMPLE: RESTAURANT SOP
The restaurant and employee references in this completed example are fictional. This example shows the level
of clarity, sequence, verification, and accountability expected in a usable SOP.
SOP TITLE
Opening the Server Beverage and Service Station
SOP Number: FOH-OPEN-004 Department: Front of House Version: 1.0
Effective Date: March 1, 2026 Owner: FOH Manager Review Frequency: Quarterly
PURPOSE
To ensure the server beverage and service station is clean, stocked, safe, and fully ready before guests are seated.
WHO PERFORMS THIS SOP
The assigned opening server completes the procedure. The opening manager verifies completion before opening the dining room.
SUPPLIES AND EQUIPMENT
□ Sanitizer bucket and test strips □ Clean wiping towels □ Cups and lids
□ Straws and napkins □ Beverage syrups □ Tea and coffee supplies
□ Ice scoop and holder □ Backup glassware □ Approved opening checklist
PROCEDURE
1. Wash hands and inspect the station for spills, food debris, pests, broken equipment, or safety hazards.
2. Prepare sanitizer at the restaurant’s approved concentration. Verify the solution with a test strip and record the result.
3. Wipe and sanitize all counters, handles, dispensers, nozzles, trays, and frequently touched surfaces.
4. Inspect beverage nozzles and tea/coffee equipment. Reclean any item that is sticky, stained, or not ready for use.
5. Stock each item to the approved opening par. Rotate dated products using first-in, first-out procedures.
6. Fill the ice bin using the approved scoop. Never use a glass or allow the scoop handle to contact the ice.
7. Brew tea and coffee according to approved recipes and record required times or discard times.
8. Test beverage equipment and report leaks, incorrect carbonation, temperature problems, or empty products immediately.
9. Remove empty boxes, personal items, and unapproved supplies from the station.
10. Complete the opening checklist and request manager verification before the first guest is seated.


COMPLETED EXAMPLE: RESTAURANT SOP - VERIFICATION
STANDARD OF COMPLETION
The station is approved only when surfaces are clean and dry, sanitizer is verified, equipment operates correctly, all required
items meet par, products are rotated and dated, no safety hazard is present, and the manager has signed the opening checklist.
COMMON ERRORS TO PREVENT
□ Stocking over dirty surfaces.
□ Preparing sanitizer without testing the concentration.
□ Using an unclean ice scoop or leaving the scoop inside the ice.
□ Mixing old and new dated products without proper rotation.
□ Marking the checklist complete before the work is actually finished.
CORRECTIVE ACTION
If any standard is missed, the employee corrects the issue before opening the station. Repeated misses require coaching,
retraining, and manager follow-up documentation.
MANAGER VERIFICATION CHECK
☒ Sanitizer tested and acceptable.
☒ Station surfaces and equipment clean.
☒ Opening pars met and products rotated.
☒ Tea, coffee, beverage, and ice procedures completed.
☒ No unresolved safety or equipment concern.
Example Completion Record: Completed by Jordan Lee at 10:12 a.m. Verified by Casey Reed at 10:18 a.m. No correction
required.
SOP REVISION RECORD
Revision Date: June 1, 2026
Change Made: Added required beverage-equipment test and manager escalation for leaks or carbonation problems.
Reason: Two opening shifts began with an empty product line that had not been identified before service.
Approved By: General Manager - Casey Reed
What makes this example strong: The employee knows who performs the task, the exact sequence, the
completion standard, the common errors, the verification method, and what happens when the standard is
missed.

PART 6
TRAINING SIGN-OFF PAGES


Purpose of This Section:
Training should not end with a verbal “they’re good.”
A restaurant needs a clear way to confirm that an employee was trained, practiced the task, demonstrated the correct standard,
and was approved by a trainer or manager.
Sign-off pages protect the employee, the trainer, the manager, and the restaurant.
They create proof that training happened.
They also help managers see where training is incomplete.
A signed page should never mean, “We showed this once.”
A signed page should mean, “This employee has been trained, observed, corrected if needed, and approved to perform this task
or position according to the restaurant standard.”
This section includes pages for:

1. Trainer sign-off standards
2. Employee training sign-off
3. Station certification
4. Position certification
5. Retraining sign-off
6. Final training approval
Managers should use these pages carefully.
Do not sign off on training that has not truly been completed.
Do not allow trainers to sign off just to move faster.
Do not certify employees who still need major correction.
A strong sign-off system helps the restaurant avoid confusion later.
If performance slips, the manager can review what was trained, when it was trained, who approved it, and what follow-up is
needed.
TRAINING SIGN-OFF RULES
□ Training must be explained clearly.
□ The employee must have a chance to practice.
□ The trainer must observe the employee performing the task.
□ Mistakes must be corrected before sign-off.
□ The employee must understand the expected standard.
□ The employee must be able to perform the task during real shift conditions when applicable.
□ The trainer must not sign off unless the employee is ready.
□ The manager should review important sign-offs.
□ Follow-up should be scheduled when the employee needs more practice.
□ Retraining should be documented when standards are missed after sign-off.
WHEN NOT TO SIGN OFF
Do not sign off if:
□ The employee only watched someone else do the task.
□ The employee still needs constant reminders.
□ The employee cannot explain the standard.
□ The employee performs the task correctly only when the restaurant is slow.
□ The employee skips safety or sanitation steps.
□ The employee does not understand when to ask for help.
□ The trainer is unsure whether the employee is ready.
□ The manager has not reviewed a critical task when manager approval is required.

PART 7
COACHING AND RETRAINING LOGS


Purpose of This Section:
Even good employees need coaching.
A restaurant should not wait until a problem becomes serious before addressing it.
Coaching gives managers a way to correct small issues early, explain expectations clearly, and help employees improve before
stronger action is needed.
Retraining gives employees a chance to relearn the correct standard when something was missed, forgotten, changed, or
misunderstood.
This section helps managers document coaching conversations, performance corrections, retraining assignments, follow-up
reviews, repeated issues, and manager action plans.
The goal is not to create paperwork for no reason.
The goal is to create fair, clear, useful documentation.
Good documentation helps answer important questions:

1. What happened?
2. What standard was missed?
3. Was the employee coached?
4. Was retraining assigned?
5. Did the manager follow up?
6. Did the issue improve?
7. Is the issue repeating?
8. What is the next action?
Coaching should be professional, specific, and connected to a clear standard.
A manager should avoid vague statements like:
“You need to do better.”
“You have to care more.”
“You’re not paying attention.”
Those statements may express frustration, but they do not clearly explain what must change.
A stronger coaching conversation sounds like:
“Tonight, the closing side work was not completed before clock-out. The server station was missing rolled silverware, the tea urns
were not cleaned, and the trash was not pulled. The closing checklist requires those items before checkout. Tomorrow, I need you
to follow the checklist and have a manager verify it before leaving.”
That kind of coaching is specific.
It names the issue.
It connects the issue to a standard.
It explains the expected correction.
It gives the employee a fair chance to improve.
WHEN COACHING IS NEEDED
Coaching may be needed when:
□ A standard is missed.
□ A task is incomplete.
□ An employee needs repeated reminders.
□ Service quality slips.
□ Food quality slips.
□ Cleaning is missed.
□ Side work is incomplete.
□ Opening or closing duties are missed.
□ Communication breaks down.
□ Guest complaints increase.
□ Safety or sanitation expectations are not followed.
□ The employee seems unsure of the correct process.
□ A small issue may become a bigger issue if ignored.
COACHING RULES FOR MANAGERS
□ Coach privately when possible.
□ Stay calm and professional.
□ Focus on the behavior or standard, not the person.
□ Explain what happened.


PART 7 - WHICH RECORD SHOULD I USE?
Do not create a stack of forms for one event. Choose the record that matches the management need, then use a follow-up page only
when verification is required.
COACHING CONVERSATION LOG
Use when: A clear standard exists and the employee needs feedback or direction.
Primary need: Routine correction or clarification
PERFORMANCE CORRECTION LOG
Use when: A standard was missed, the issue is repeated, or the matter needs a formal operating record.
Primary need: Formal performance correction
RETRAINING ASSIGNMENT LOG
Use when: The employee needs the correct method taught, practiced, and re-observed.
Primary need: Skill or knowledge gap
FOLLOW-UP REVIEW PAGE
Use when: A prior coaching, correction, or retraining action has a due date and must be checked.
Primary need: Verify whether action worked
REPEATED ISSUE TRACKER
Use when: The manager needs to see the pattern, causes, and prior actions in one place.
Primary need: Same issue keeps returning
MANAGER ACTION PLAN
Use when: The correction requires coordinated steps rather than one training event.
Primary need: Several actions, owners, or deadlines
DOCUMENTATION STANDARD

Record observable facts, the exact standard, the employee's response, support offered, the next action, due date, and how completion will be
verified. Avoid diagnosing attitude, intent, personality, or motive when the evidence only shows behavior or performance.
EMPLOYMENT / HR BOUNDARY

These tools support operational coaching and training documentation. They do not determine disciplinary level, termination, leave, accommodation,
protected activity, or other employment-law decisions. Follow company policy and obtain qualified HR or legal guidance when required.

PART 8
30/60/90 DAY EMPLOYEE REVIEWS


Purpose of This Section:
The first 90 days are one of the most important periods in an employee’s time with the restaurant.
This is when managers should confirm whether the employee understands the position, follows standards, fits the team, responds
to coaching, and continues improving.
A 30/60/90 day review system gives managers a structured way to check progress before small issues become long-term habits.
These reviews should not be treated like punishment.
They should be treated as checkpoints.
A good review tells the employee:

1. What they are doing well
2. Where they still need improvement
3. What standards matter most
4. What the manager has observed
5. What the next goal is
6. What support or training is still needed
Managers should use these reviews to be honest, fair, and specific.
Avoid vague review comments like:
“You’re doing fine.”
“Needs to improve.”
“Good attitude.”
“Not quite there yet.”
Those comments do not give the employee enough direction.
Stronger review comments sound like:
“You are greeting tables quickly and your guest tone is strong. The next focus is order accuracy, especially modifiers and seat
numbers.”
“You are improving on station setup, but closing duties still need manager reminders. For the next 30 days, we will focus on
completing the closing checklist before clock-out.”
“You work well with the team, but food labeling has been missed twice. We reviewed the labeling SOP today and will follow up
next week.”
The purpose of a review is not just to judge performance.
The purpose is to create the next step.


30/60/90-DAY REVIEW - EVIDENCE & RATING CALIBRATION
Use the same role standard for every employee. Ratings should describe observed performance during the review period - not
popularity, personality, one unusually good shift, or one unusually bad shift.
NEEDS IMPROVEMENT

Performance is below the current communicated standard or still requires more support than the role normally allows. Document a specific example
and next action.
ACCEPTABLE

Performance meets the current standard with normal supervision for the role. The employee is dependable at the reviewed level.
STRONG

Performance meets the standard consistently, including normal pressure, with little repeated prompting. Strength should be supported by an
example.
EXCELLENT

Use only when the employee consistently exceeds the role standard in observable ways without bypassing procedure or creating risk for others.
NOT APPLICABLE

The category does not apply to this role or has not been observed enough to rate fairly.
FAIR REVIEW RULES

Use examples and evidence.
Compare the employee to the role standard, not to a favorite employee.
Separate a training gap from a process, equipment, staffing, communication, or accountability problem.
Do not wait for a scheduled review to address a serious safety, harassment, discrimination, cash, or conduct concern.
Allow the employee to respond and document the response fairly.
End every improvement rating with one owner, one action, one due date, and one follow-up method.

MANAGER TRAINING ACCOUNTABILITY


PART 9 - MANAGER TRAINING ACCOUNTABILITY
Training accountability should control real risk, not create paperwork for its own sake. Use the lightest review rhythm that still gives
management a truthful picture of readiness and open follow-up.
MINIMUM VIABLE RHYTHM

Active training week: Complete a weekly review when there is an active trainee, overdue follow-up, trainer concern, certification decision, or
repeated training gap.
Monthly system check: Review training completion, certification coverage, overdue follow-up, repeated issues, trainer calibration, and SOPs that
need correction.
No active trainees: Do not manufacture weekly paperwork. Verify the system is ready before the next hire and close any open actions.
Small restaurant: One manager may combine weekly and department reviews into a single control page.
Larger restaurant: Department leads may review their areas, but one GM/owner-level review should reconcile unresolved risks and conflicting
standards.
DO NOT COMPLETE EVERY PART 9 FORM EVERY WEEK

Weekly Review = active people and due actions. Monthly Review = trends and system health. Department Scorecard = one department that needs a
deeper look. Training Gaps Review = a specific recurring gap. Team Readiness Review = upcoming shift/event readiness. Manager Accountability
Checklist = periodic manager-system verification.
MANAGER ACCOUNTABILITY RULES

Confirm employees are not scheduled independently before verified readiness.
Confirm trainers use the current standard and sign off honestly.
Review open actions until they are closed or reassigned.
Treat repeated mistakes as evidence to diagnose - not automatic proof of an attitude problem.
Update training or SOPs when the restaurant changes the menu, equipment, process, or role.
Escalate serious safety, harassment, discrimination, injury, allergy, cash, or conduct concerns immediately according to policy.

FINAL WORKBOOK CLOSING & CONTROL
A training system is successful only when it changes what people can reliably do during real restaurant work. The goal is not
completed paperwork. The goal is clearer standards, stronger employees, fairer accountability, and fewer preventable surprises.
TRUTH - VERIFY WHAT IS REAL

Observe performance in real conditions.
Use examples, counts, completed work, guest/quality evidence, and current records.
Distinguish a training gap from a process, staffing, equipment, communication, or accountability problem.
Do not certify readiness because time passed or because someone is well liked.
COST - NAME WHY THE GAP MATTERS

Connect important training failures to the consequence they create: manager time, labor, waste, remakes, missed sales, turnover, safety,
sanitation, guest trust, employee trust, or operational instability. Do not invent a dollar amount when the restaurant does not have evidence for
one.
SYSTEM - CLOSE THE LOOP

Name the standard and assign one owner.
Set the next action and due date.
Provide practice or support when needed.
Verify performance after the action.
Update the SOP, checklist, trainer authorization, or training plan when the system caused the gap.
