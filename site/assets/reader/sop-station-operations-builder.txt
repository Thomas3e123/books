HOW TO USE THIS WORKBOOK
This workbook is a practical operating system. It is meant to be written in, customized for your restaurant, reproduced within the
internal-use license, and used during real manager follow-up.
For best results, use the workbook in this order:
1. Read the SOP foundation pages before editing templates.
2. Identify the highest-risk and highest-frequency operating failures first.
3. Customize each SOP to match your actual building, equipment, menu, systems, staffing, and policies.
4. Assign an SOP identification number, version, owner, approval date, and review date.
5. Turn critical SOPs into short station posters, checklists, and verification forms.
6. Train employees from the complete SOP, not only from the checklist or poster.
7. Observe employees during real shift conditions before signing them off.
8. Use manager verification forms to confirm that the standard is being followed.
9. Review, revise, retire, and replace SOPs through a controlled approval process.
10. Store completed records securely and follow restaurant and legal retention requirements.
The purpose of this workbook is not to create paperwork. The purpose is to create clarity, consistency, training structure,
document control, and manager accountability.


QUICK-START SOP IMPLEMENTATION GUIDE
Use this guide to move from “we need better systems” to a controlled, trainable SOP program without trying to implement the
entire workbook at once.
STEP 1 - NAME THE SOP SYSTEM OWNER
Assign one manager to maintain the master index, version control, approval records, training status, and scheduled reviews.
STEP 2 - SELECT THE FIRST FIVE SOPs
Begin with the procedures that carry the greatest safety, financial, guest-experience, or operational risk. Do not begin with the
easiest documents simply because they are easy.
STEP 3 - CUSTOMIZE BEFORE TRAINING
Confirm the procedure matches the restaurant’s actual layout, equipment, menu, staffing, technology, policies, and emergency
resources.
STEP 4 - APPROVE THE CONTROLLED VERSION
Assign the SOP ID, version, effective date, department owner, approver, review date, and change summary before it is released.
STEP 5 - BUILD THE SUPPORTING TOOLS
Create the station poster, checklist, log, sign-off page, and manager verification form needed to make the SOP usable during a
real shift.

QUICK-START GUIDE - TRAIN, VERIFY, AND MAINTAIN
STEP 6 - TRAIN FOR UNDERSTANDING
Explain the purpose, demonstrate the process, allow practice, observe performance, correct errors, and confirm that the employee
can explain the critical standards.
STEP 7 - VERIFY DURING REAL OPERATIONS
A classroom explanation is not enough. Verify the standard during opening, service, closing, rush conditions, or the actual
situation the SOP controls.
STEP 8 - DOCUMENT THE RESULT
Complete the training sign-off, manager verification, correction record, or incident form. Verbal follow-up alone does not create a
reliable operating record.
STEP 9 - REVIEW THE FIRST WEEK
Check whether employees can find the SOP, whether the poster and checklist are useful, whether managers are verifying it, and
whether the procedure needs clarification.
STEP 10 - PLACE THE SOP ON A REVIEW CYCLE
Review after incidents, repeated failures, equipment or menu changes, policy changes, and at the scheduled review date.

THE GRAVITY SOP TEST
TRUTH -> COST -> SYSTEM
Before creating or revising an SOP, work through these three questions in order. A system should solve a real restaurant problem.
Paperwork is not the goal.
TRUTH - What is actually happening?
Name the recurring failure, inconsistency, risk, confusion, or missed standard. Use observable facts: what is happening, where it
happens, how often it happens, and what evidence shows the problem is real.
COST - What does the truth cost if it continues?
Identify the consequence in safety, time, ticket speed, labor, waste, food cost, quality, guest trust, team trust, revenue,
compliance, or manager attention. Prioritize recurring and serious costs over minor inconvenience.
SYSTEM - What is the smallest repeatable control that addresses the cost?
Choose only what is needed: a controlled SOP, station poster, checklist, form or log, training step, manager verification, review
cycle, or a combination of those tools. Assign an owner, approval, training, verification, and follow-up.
GRAVITY RULE
Do not create a form because a form can exist. Build the smallest system that prevents, catches, or corrects a real cost.

Purpose of This Section
A restaurant cannot run consistently if every employee is working from memory, habit, opinion, or whatever they were
shown on their first few shifts.
Standard Operating Procedures, also called SOPs, give a restaurant a written way to explain how important tasks should
be completed.
An SOP does not replace good management.
An SOP helps management become clearer.
It gives employees a standard to learn from. It gives trainers a standard to teach from. It gives managers a standard to
verify. It gives owners a way to see whether the restaurant is operating with structure or guesswork.
Many restaurants have talented employees but weak systems.
One cook may set up the line one way. Another cook may set it up differently. One server may greet tables quickly. Another
may wait too long. One host may seat by rotation. Another may seat based on habit. One dishwasher may keep the dish pit
flowing. Another may let dirty dishes pile up until the whole restaurant feels the pressure.
The problem is not always effort.
The problem is often that the correct process was never written down, trained, posted, checked, and followed up.
This workbook is designed to help managers build SOPs that can actually be used during real restaurant operations.
Each SOP should answer these questions:
What is the task?
Why does it matter?
Who is responsible?
When should it be done?
What tools or supplies are needed?
What safety or sanitation rules apply?
What steps must be followed?
What does “done correctly” look like?
What mistakes should be avoided?
How will a manager verify the standard?
When should the SOP be reviewed?
The goal is not to make restaurant work complicated.
The goal is to make repeated tasks clear.
Clear systems reduce confusion, protect standards, improve training, strengthen accountability, and help the restaurant run
with more consistency.


1. Why Restaurant SOPs Matter
Restaurants are busy, fast-moving, people-driven businesses.
During a shift, employees make hundreds of small decisions. They decide how to set up a station, how to greet a guest,
how to enter an order, how to label food, how to clean equipment, how to handle a complaint, how to close a station, and
how to respond when something goes wrong.
When those decisions are not guided by clear standards, the restaurant becomes inconsistent.
Inconsistency shows up in many ways.
Guests receive different service depending on who is working.
Food looks different depending on which cook is on the line.
Closing duties are missed because no one follows the same checklist.
Prep levels change because each employee guesses differently.
Cleaning standards slip because “clean” means different things to different people.
Managers keep correcting the same problems.
New employees learn different methods from different trainers.
A written SOP helps reduce those problems.
An SOP creates one clear standard for a task or process.
It does not have to be complicated. A strong SOP is simple, specific, practical, and easy to train from.
A restaurant SOP should help an employee understand exactly what to do and why it matters.
The “why” is important.
Employees are more likely to follow a standard when they understand the reason behind it. A server who understands that
a quick greeting protects the guest’s first impression is more likely to take the greeting seriously. A prep cook who
understands that proper labeling protects food safety and reduces waste is more likely to label correctly. A dishwasher who
understands that clean dish flow affects the entire restaurant is more likely to prioritize the right items during a rush.
SOPs also protect managers.
A manager cannot fairly hold employees accountable to a standard that has never been written, explained, trained, and
verified.
Clear SOPs make accountability more professional.
A manager can say:
“This is the standard.”
“This is why it matters.”
“This is how we trained it.”
“This is what needs to improve.”
“This is when we will follow up.”
That is stronger than correcting employees based on memory, frustration, or personal opinion.
A strong restaurant does not rely only on good employees.
A strong restaurant builds systems that help good employees succeed and help struggling employees improve.

2. What a Restaurant SOP Is
A restaurant SOP is a written standard for how a task, station, process, or situation should be handled.
An SOP should be clear enough that a manager can train from it, an employee can follow it, and another manager can
verify it.
A good SOP does not simply say:
“Clean the station.”
That is too vague.
A better SOP explains what clean means.
It may include:
What surfaces must be wiped
What products must be stored
What tools must be washed
What floors must be swept and mopped
What trash must be removed
What labels must be checked
What the manager should inspect before the employee leaves
A good SOP turns unclear expectations into visible standards.
SOPs can be used for many parts of restaurant operations, including:
Host stand procedures
Server service steps
Bar setup and closing
Cook line station setup
Recipe execution
Prep procedures
Dish pit flow
Cleaning tasks
Sanitation standards


Opening and closing duties
Guest complaint response
Manager walkthroughs
Cash handling
Labor cut decisions
Emergency situations
An SOP is not only a rule.
It is a training tool.
It helps new employees learn the job correctly. It helps trainers stay consistent. It helps experienced employees stay
aligned. It helps managers identify whether a problem is a training issue, a system issue, or an accountability issue.
An SOP should not be written once and forgotten.
Restaurant operations change. Menus change. Equipment changes. Staffing changes. Health department requirements
may change. Service standards may change. When the restaurant changes, SOPs must be reviewed and updated.
A weak SOP sits in a binder and is ignored.
A strong SOP is used during training, posted where employees need it, checked by managers, and updated when the
operation changes.

3. What Every SOP Should Include
A strong SOP should be easy to understand and easy to use.
It should not be written like a legal document. It should be written like an operational tool.
Every SOP in this workbook should include the same basic structure so managers and employees know what to expect.
The most important parts are:


SOP Title
The name of the task, station, or process.
Department
The area of the restaurant connected to the SOP.
Station
The exact station or work area where the SOP applies.
Purpose
A short explanation of what the SOP is designed to accomplish.
Why It Matters
The reason the task affects the restaurant, the guest, the team, safety, food quality, cost, or accountability.
Who Performs This Task
The position responsible for doing the work.
When It Is Performed
The time, shift, situation, or frequency when the task should be completed.
Tools / Equipment Needed
The supplies, equipment, forms, or systems needed to complete the task.
Safety or Sanitation Notes
Any food safety, personal safety, chemical safety, allergy, equipment, or health department concerns.
Step-by-Step Procedure
The correct order of steps.
Quality Standard
What the task should look like when completed correctly.
Common Mistakes to Avoid
The errors that happen most often and should be prevented.
Manager Verification
How a manager, trainer, or lead will confirm the SOP was followed correctly.
Training Sign-Off
Confirmation that the employee was trained and can perform the task.
Review Date
The date the SOP should be reviewed or updated.
The “Why It Matters” section should never be skipped.
This section helps employees understand that the SOP is not just paperwork. It connects the task to real restaurant
outcomes.
For example:
A handwashing SOP matters because it protects guests, employees, and food safety.
A table greeting SOP matters because it shapes the guest’s first impression.
A line setup SOP matters because poor setup slows ticket times and increases mistakes.
A power outage SOP matters because lighting, refrigeration, cooking equipment, payment systems, and guest safety may
all be affected.
When employees understand why a procedure matters, they are more likely to take ownership of the standard.

4. How to Write SOPs Employees Can Actually Follow
A restaurant SOP only works if employees can understand it and managers can train from it.


If the SOP is too long, too vague, too complicated, or too disconnected from the real shift, employees will ignore it.
The best SOPs are clear, direct, and practical.
Use simple language.
Instead of writing:
“Ensure the guest-facing environment remains presentable.”
Write:
“Keep the host stand clean, organized, and free of trash, old menus, personal drinks, and unnecessary items.”
Use action words.
Start steps with words like:
Check
Clean
Stock
Label
Remove
Notify
Record
Wash
Sanitize
Verify
Store
Lock
Turn off
Report
Avoid vague words when possible.
Words like properly, regularly, clean, ready, good, and organized can mean different things to different employees.
Be specific.
Instead of:
“Restock the server station.”
Write:
“Restock napkins, straws, receipt paper, clean menus, to-go boxes, silverware, and condiments before the shift begins.”
Put steps in the correct order.
Restaurant work often depends on sequence. A prep cook should not label product after it has already been stored and
forgotten. A cook should not start service without checking station backup. A manager should not lock the building before
completing the final walkthrough.
Include standards that can be checked.
A manager should be able to look at the task and know whether it was done correctly.
For example:
All food containers are labeled with product name, prep date, use-by date, and employee initials.
All clean dishes are dry, free of food residue, and stored in the correct location.
All server stations are stocked before the first guest is seated.
All cash drawers are counted and verified before the employee leaves.
Include the common mistakes.
Many SOPs become stronger when they explain what usually goes wrong.
Common mistakes help managers prevent repeat problems before they happen.
A good SOP should not embarrass employees. It should help them succeed.

5. How to Customize SOPs for Your Restaurant
No two restaurants operate exactly the same way.
Even restaurants with similar menus may have different layouts, equipment, staffing levels, table numbers, storage areas,
POS systems, service styles, hours, recipes, cleaning schedules, and management expectations.
That is why SOP templates must be customized.
This workbook gives you a strong starting point, but each SOP should be adjusted before it becomes an official restaurant
standard.
A server SOP should match your service style.
A bar SOP should match your drink menu, alcohol rules, glassware, storage areas, and responsible alcohol service policy.
A cook line SOP should match your equipment, menu items, station layout, portion tools, recipe cards, and ticket system.
A prep SOP should match your par levels, labeling system, storage areas, shelf-life standards, and production needs.
A dish SOP should match your dish machine, rack system, chemical setup, dish pit layout, and closing duties.
A manager SOP should match your authority structure, cash handling rules, labor expectations, incident reporting process,
and owner communication standards.
Emergency SOPs require special attention.
Emergency procedures must be customized to match your building layout, shelter areas, exits, alarm system, emergency
lighting, local weather risks, fire department guidance, health department requirements, company policy, insurance
requirements, and local law.
A template is not enough for emergency planning.
Ownership and management should review emergency SOPs carefully and make sure employees know what to do before
an emergency happens.
Customization makes the SOP real.
A generic SOP may sound good on paper.
A customized SOP can actually be trained, posted, followed, and verified.

9. SOP Training and Sign-Off Process
An SOP does not help the restaurant if employees are never trained on it.
Posting an SOP is not the same as training an SOP.
Handing someone a checklist is not the same as confirming they understand the task.
Managers and trainers should use a clear process when training employees on SOPs.
The process should be:
Explain the SOP.
Explain why it matters.
Demonstrate the task.
Let the employee practice.
Observe the employee during real work.
Correct mistakes immediately.
Have the employee repeat the task.
Verify consistency.
Document sign-off.
Schedule follow-up if needed.
Employees should not be signed off just because they heard the explanation.
They should be signed off when they can perform the task correctly and consistently during real restaurant conditions.
For example, a host should not be signed off on seating rotation only because they understand the chart.
They should be observed seating guests during an actual shift.
A cook should not be signed off on line setup only because they watched someone else set up.
They should complete the setup correctly, using the right tools, product, portions, labels, and sanitation standards.
A manager should not be signed off on closing procedures only because they read the closing checklist.
They should complete a full closing walkthrough and verify the building, cash, equipment, food storage, security, and notes.
Training should always connect the written SOP to real performance.
The goal is not paperwork.
The goal is consistent execution.

10. SOP Review and Update System
A restaurant SOP should not be permanent without review.
Operations change.
A menu item may change.
A station may be moved.
A new piece of equipment may be added.


A health department requirement may need attention.
A manager may identify a better process.
An emergency may reveal a gap.
A repeated mistake may show that the SOP is unclear.
When something changes, the SOP should be reviewed.
SOPs should also be reviewed on a regular schedule.
Some SOPs may need monthly review, especially safety, sanitation, cash handling, emergency procedures, and manager
closing duties.
Other SOPs may only need quarterly or semiannual review.
The important thing is that someone owns the review process.
A good SOP review should ask:
Is this SOP still accurate?
Is the process still being followed?
Are employees trained on it?
Is the station poster still posted?
Is the checklist being used?
Are managers verifying completion?
Are there repeated mistakes?
Does the SOP need clearer wording?
Does the SOP need to be retrained?
Reviewing SOPs keeps the system alive.
A binder full of outdated procedures can create confusion.
A reviewed and updated SOP system creates structure.
The manager’s job is not just to write SOPs.
The manager’s job is to keep them useful.

HOST STAND STANDARDS
The host stand controls the first impression of the restaurant.
WHY IT MATTERS
Guests begin judging the restaurant before they reach the table. A strong host stand helps guests feel welcomed, keeps

KEY STANDARDS
□ Greet every guest quickly.
□ Make eye contact and use a professional tone.
□ Confirm party size clearly.
□ Ask about seating needs or special requests.
□ Quote wait times honestly.
□ Seat guests by rotation whenever possible.
□ Communicate delays before guests become frustrated.
□ Keep the host stand clean and organized.
□ Watch the front door and waiting area.
□ Notify a manager when a guest is upset, confused, or waiting too long.
NEVER
□ Ignore guests at the door.
□ Guess at wait times without checking.
□ Double-seat servers without a reason.
□ Leave the host stand messy.
□ Argue with guests about seating.

Purpose of This Section
MODEL-SOP SCOPE: This Part intentionally contains 5 representative SOPs: Host Stand Opening SOP; Guest Greeting SOP; Server
Order Taking SOP; Table Maintenance SOP; Guest Complaint SOP. Use them to study structure, customization, training, verification, and
document control. Additional department procedures are reserved for The Gravity Restaurant SOP Library.
Front of house standards affect the guest experience from the moment a guest walks in until the moment they leave.
The front of house team controls first impressions, table flow, service pace, order accuracy, guest communication, dining
room cleanliness, and guest recovery. When FOH procedures are unclear, service becomes inconsistent. One host may
greet guests warmly while another barely looks up. One server may enter orders carefully while another forgets modifiers.
One employee may keep tables clean while another lets plates stack up.
Front of house SOPs help reduce those differences.
A strong FOH SOP explains what the employee should do, why it matters, when the task should happen, what the standard
looks like, what mistakes to avoid, and how a manager will verify the task.
These SOPs are designed as templates.
Each restaurant should customize them to match its service style, table layout, POS system, menu, staffing structure,
alcohol policies, reservation process, side work standards, guest recovery rules, and management expectations.
Use these SOPs during onboarding, position training, station readiness verification, coaching, retraining, and manager
follow-up.
Do not treat an SOP as complete until employees have been trained, observed, corrected if needed, and signed off during
real shift conditions.
The goal of this section is simple:
Create a front of house operation where guests are welcomed quickly, seated correctly, served consistently, communicated
with professionally, and recovered properly when something goes wrong.

Department: Front of House
Station / Process: Host Stand
Purpose:
To make sure the host stand is clean, stocked, organized, and ready before guests arrive.
Why It Matters:
The host stand is often the first place guests interact with the restaurant. If the host stand is messy, unprepared, or
disorganized, the restaurant feels unprofessional before service even begins. A ready host stand helps guests feel
welcomed, helps seating run smoothly, supports server rotation, and gives the manager a stronger start to the shift.
Who Performs This Task:
Host, opening host, shift lead, or manager on duty.
When It Is Performed:
Before the restaurant opens or before the host begins seating guests.
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Menus

Phone

Manager communication method if applicable
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Menus should be clean and in good condition.
Walkways near the host stand should be clear.
High chairs and booster seats should be clean and safe.
Electrical cords, mats, or clutter should not create a trip hazard.
Step-by-Step Procedure:

1. Arrive at the host stand before the scheduled opening time.
2. Check that the host stand is clean and free of trash, old notes, personal drinks, unnecessary paper, and clutter.

3. Confirm that menus are clean, organized, and ready for guests.
4. Check that kids menus, to-go menus, drink menus, or special menus are available if used by the restaurant.

5. Review the seating chart or table map.
6. Review server sections for the shift.
7. Review reservation notes, large parties, special events, or expected guest flow.
8. Confirm that the waitlist system or reservation system is ready.
9. Make sure pens, paper, phone, and other host supplies are available.
10. Check the front entrance and waiting area for cleanliness.
11. Confirm that high chairs and booster seats are clean and accessible if applicable.
12. Notify the manager of missing supplies, seating concerns, reservation issues, or guest area problems before service
begins.
Quality Standard:


The host stand is clean, stocked, organized, and ready before the first guest arrives. The host understands the seating
chart, server sections, reservation needs, and manager expectations for the shift.
Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Leaving old notes or clutter at the host stand.
Starting the shift without reviewing server sections.
Failing to check reservations or large party notes.
Using dirty or damaged menus.
Waiting until guests arrive to fix host stand problems.
Not telling the manager about missing supplies or seating concerns.

SOP Title: Guest Greeting SOP
Department: Front of House
Station / Process: Host Stand / Guest Arrival
Purpose:
To make sure every guest is greeted quickly, professionally, and consistently when entering the restaurant.
Why It Matters:
The guest greeting sets the tone for the entire visit. If guests feel ignored, rushed, or unwelcome at the door, the restaurant
may already be trying to recover before service begins. A strong greeting helps guests feel noticed, creates a professional
first impression, and supports smoother seating and communication.
Who Performs This Task:
Host, server, bartender, manager, or any employee near the entrance when a guest arrives.
When It Is Performed:
Immediately when a guest enters or approaches the host stand.
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Host stand
Seating chart
Reservation system if applicable
Waitlist system if applicable
Menus
Professional communication
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Keep the entrance clear.
Do not block walkways while greeting guests.
Assist guests with mobility needs according to restaurant policy.
Step-by-Step Procedure:

10. Communicate clearly and calmly if there is a delay.
11. Thank the guest for waiting if applicable.
12. Notify the manager if the guest is upset, confused, waiting too long, or has a special concern.

Every guest should feel noticed quickly, greeted professionally, and clearly informed about the next step.

Ignoring guests while looking at the seating chart or phone.
Using a careless or rushed tone.
Guessing wait times.
Failing to confirm party size.
Failing to ask about reservation status when required.

Arguing with guests about wait time or seating.

To make sure guest orders are taken clearly, accurately, and professionally.

Who Performs This Task:
Server, bartender, or approved employee taking guest orders.

Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:

10. Enter the order into the POS accurately.
11. Review the order before sending it.

Guessing about ingredients.

SOP Title: Guest Complaint SOP
Department: Front of House / Management
Station / Process: Guest Recovery
Purpose:
To handle guest complaints calmly, professionally, and consistently.
Why It Matters:
Guest complaints are opportunities to recover trust. Poor complaint handling can turn one issue into a lost guest, a bad
review, employee conflict, or a manager escalation. A clear complaint SOP helps employees respond without arguing,
blaming, guessing, or making promises they are not authorized to make.
Who Performs This Task:
Server, host, bartender, manager, shift lead, or any employee who receives a guest concern.
When It Is Performed:
Any time a guest expresses dissatisfaction, concern, frustration, complaint, or serious question about service, food,
payment, safety, or experience.
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Professional communication
Manager support
Guest complaint form if needed
POS access if manager correction is required
Restaurant guest recovery policy
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Escalate food safety, allergy, illness, injury, alcohol, security, or payment complaints to a manager immediately.
Do not admit fault for injuries or serious incidents.
Follow company policy for documentation.
Step-by-Step Procedure:

3. Do not interrupt.
4. Apologize professionally.
5. Do not blame another employee or department.

8. Thank the guest for bringing the issue forward.
9. Notify a manager when required.
10. Correct what can be corrected quickly and within policy.
11. Follow manager direction for comps, remakes, refunds, or follow-up.
12. Document the complaint when required.
13. Watch for repeated complaint patterns.
Quality Standard:
The guest feels heard, the issue is handled professionally, and the manager is involved when needed.
Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Arguing with the guest.
Blaming the kitchen, host, server, or manager.
Making unauthorized promises.
Ignoring the complaint.


Waiting too long to notify a manager.
Acting defensive.
Failing to document serious or repeated complaints.

□ Employee listens calmly.
□ Employee apologizes professionally.

PART 4
BAR MODEL SOPS, CHECKLISTS & FORMS


Purpose of This Section
MODEL-SOP SCOPE: This Part intentionally contains 4 representative SOPs: Bar Opening SOP; Drink Preparation SOP; Responsible
Alcohol Service SOP; Bar Closing SOP. Use them to study structure, customization, training, verification, and document control.
Additional department procedures are reserved for The Gravity Restaurant SOP Library.
The bar is one of the highest-impact stations in a full-service restaurant.
A strong bar can improve guest experience, increase sales, support servers, protect alcohol control, maintain product
consistency, and help the restaurant feel professional and organized.
A weak bar can create major problems.
Drinks may be made inconsistently. Guests may wait too long. Alcohol may be over-poured. IDs may not be checked
correctly. Product may be wasted. Service well tickets may back up. The bar may close poorly and create problems for the
next shift.
Bar SOPs help protect the restaurant by making expectations clear.
These templates are designed to help managers create consistent standards for bar opening, guest service, drink
preparation, responsible alcohol service, refusal of service, service well flow, cash handling, cleaning, and closing.
Each restaurant must customize these SOPs to match its own bar layout, drink menu, POS system, alcohol service laws,
company policies, glassware, product storage, staffing structure, tip procedures, and manager approval requirements.
Alcohol-related SOPs must always follow federal, state, and local laws, licensing requirements, company policy, and
responsible alcohol service training requirements.
The goal of this section is not only to improve drink service.
The goal is to build a bar system that is fast, clean, consistent, responsible, profitable, and manager-verifiable.

SOP Title: Responsible Alcohol Service SOP
Department: Bar / Front of House / Management
Station / Process: Alcohol Service
Purpose:
To make sure alcohol is served legally, responsibly, and according to restaurant policy.
Why It Matters:
Alcohol service creates serious responsibility for the restaurant, the bartender, the server, the manager, and the guest.
Poor alcohol control can lead to legal problems, unsafe situations, guest injury, over-service, underage service, license risk,
and damage to the restaurant’s reputation.
Who Performs This Task:
Bartender, server, manager, or any employee approved to serve alcohol.
When It Is Performed:
Any time alcohol is requested, ordered, prepared, served, monitored, refused, or questioned.
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Responsible alcohol service training
Valid ID checking procedure
Manager support
Alcohol service policy
Incident form if needed
POS system
Refusal procedure
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Follow all federal, state, and local alcohol laws.
Follow company policy.
Check IDs according to policy.
Do not serve underage guests.
Do not serve visibly intoxicated guests.
Notify a manager when alcohol safety concerns arise.
Step-by-Step Procedure:

1. Follow restaurant alcohol service policy at all times.
2. Check ID according to policy and law.
3. Confirm the ID appears valid, current, and belongs to the guest.
4. Refuse service if ID is missing, invalid, questionable, or does not meet requirements.
5. Monitor guest behavior and pace of drinking.
6. Watch for signs of intoxication.
7. Communicate concerns to a manager early.
8. Stop or slow service when directed by manager or policy.
9. Do not argue with a guest about alcohol refusal.
10. Do not serve alcohol to another guest if it appears intended for someone who should not be served.

11. Document alcohol incidents according to restaurant policy.
12. Support safe guest departure according to manager direction and law.
Quality Standard:
Alcohol is served only to eligible guests, monitored responsibly, and refused when required by law or policy.
Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Assuming a guest is old enough without checking ID when required.
Accepting questionable ID.


Ignoring signs of intoxication.
Continuing service because the guest is spending money.
Arguing with a guest about refusal.
Serving alcohol through another guest.
Failing to notify the manager.
Failing to document serious incidents.
Manager Verification:
Manager or trainer should verify that:
□ Employee understands alcohol policy.
□ Employee knows ID checking procedure.
□ Employee recognizes warning signs.
□ Employee knows when to refuse service.
□ Employee knows when to involve manager.
□ Employee understands documentation requirements.
□ Employee follows local law and company policy.

MODEL-SOP SCOPE: This Part intentionally contains 5 representative SOPs: Cook Line Opening SOP; Station Setup SOP; Recipe

The cook line is one of the most important operating areas in the restaurant.
The line affects food quality, ticket times, food cost, waste, guest satisfaction, kitchen morale, and the restaurant’s
reputation. When the cook line is organized, stocked, clean, and communicating well, the entire restaurant runs stronger.
When the cook line is unprepared, inconsistent, or poorly managed, problems spread quickly.
Tickets slow down.
Food leaves the kitchen incorrectly.
Servers wait longer.
Guests become frustrated.
Managers spend the shift reacting.
Food waste increases.
Employees blame each other.
Cook line SOPs help prevent those problems by making expectations clear before service begins.
These templates are designed to help managers create standards for cook line opening, station setup, recipe execution,
portion control, ticket reading, rush communication, food quality checks, allergy orders, restocking, cleaning, and closing.
Each restaurant should customize these SOPs to match its own menu, recipes, equipment, station layout, ticket system,
expo process, food safety procedures, portion tools, cleaning standards, and management expectations.
The goal of this section is to help managers build a cook line that is prepared, consistent, clean, communicative, and
accountable during real restaurant conditions.

Department: Back of House
Station / Process: Cook Line Opening
Purpose:
To make sure the cook line is clean, stocked, organized, safe, and ready before service begins.
Why It Matters:
A weak line opening creates problems that last through the shift. Missing product, dirty equipment, poor setup, low backup,
and unclear station readiness slow ticket times and increase mistakes. A strong opening gives cooks and managers control
before orders begin.
Who Performs This Task:
Opening cook, station cook, kitchen lead, sous chef, shift lead, or manager on duty.
When It Is Performed:
Before meal service begins, before tickets start, or before the assigned cook begins working the station.

Check that equipment is operating safely.
Report unsafe equipment immediately.
Follow food safety, allergy, and cross-contamination standards.
Step-by-Step Procedure:

Quality Standard:

helps managers verify readiness quickly.
Who Performs This Task:

SOP Title: Allergy Order SOP
Department: Front of House / Back of House / Management
Station / Process: Allergy Order Handling
Purpose:
To make sure allergy orders are communicated, prepared, verified, and served with serious attention and caution.
Why It Matters:
Food allergies can be serious and may be life-threatening. Allergy mistakes can harm guests, damage trust, create legal
risk, and put the restaurant in a dangerous situation. A clear allergy SOP helps employees avoid guessing and follow a
careful communication and verification process.
Who Performs This Task:
Server, bartender, host if receiving information, cook, expo, kitchen lead, manager, or any employee involved in the order.
When It Is Performed:
Any time a guest mentions an allergy, food sensitivity, ingredient concern, or cross-contact concern.
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Allergy policy
POS allergy notes
Manager communication
Clean tools
Clean surfaces
Recipe / ingredient information
Kitchen communication
Allergy order verification form if used
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Do not guess about ingredients.
Do not treat allergies like preferences.
Prevent cross-contact according to restaurant policy.
Use clean tools and surfaces when required.
Notify a manager according to policy.
Do not serve the item if there is uncertainty.
Step-by-Step Procedure:

1. Take the guest’s allergy statement seriously.
2. Clarify the allergy concern without guessing.
3. Notify the manager according to restaurant policy.
4. Enter allergy notes clearly in the POS or order system.
5. Communicate the allergy to the kitchen, expo, or manager.
6. Confirm whether the item can be safely prepared according to restaurant policy.
7. Use clean tools, surfaces, pans, gloves, or procedures required by policy.
8. Prevent cross-contact as required.
9. Verify the order before it leaves the kitchen.
10. Communicate clearly to the server or manager.
11. Serve only when the order has been verified.
12. If uncertain, stop and ask the manager before serving.
Quality Standard:
Allergy concerns are taken seriously, communicated clearly, prepared according to policy, verified before service, and
never guessed.


Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Guessing about ingredients.
Failing to tell the manager.
Entering unclear allergy notes.
Treating allergy as a preference.
Ignoring cross-contact risk.
Using contaminated tools or surfaces.
Serving an item when uncertain.
Failing to verify before service.
Manager Verification:

□ Allergy was communicated clearly.
□ POS / ticket note was clear.
□ Kitchen understood the concern.
□ Required procedures were followed.
□ Cross-contact risk was addressed.

□ Employee knows not to guess.

gets wasted. Portions become inconsistent. Ticket times slow down. Managers spend the shift reacting to problems that
should have been prevented earlier.
Prep SOPs help protect food quality, food safety, ticket times, waste control, inventory accuracy, and kitchen consistency.
These templates are designed to help managers create standards for prep opening, prep list completion, produce prep,
protein prep, sauce and dressing prep, portioning, labeling and dating, FIFO rotation, prep waste, and prep closing.
Each restaurant should customize these SOPs to match its own menu, recipes, par levels, production schedule, storage
areas, equipment, shelf-life standards, food safety requirements, and manager expectations.
The goal of this section is to help managers build a prep system that is accurate, clean, organized, food-safe, waste-
conscious, and ready to support real service volume.

SOP Title: FIFO Rotation SOP
Department: Back of House / Storage
Station / Process: Product Rotation
Purpose:
To make sure older product is used before newer product according to FIFO standards.
Why It Matters:
FIFO means first in, first out. Proper rotation helps reduce waste, protect food safety, improve product quality, prevent
expired product, and support inventory accuracy. Poor rotation causes spoilage, unnecessary food cost, and health
inspection risk.
Who Performs This Task:
Prep cook, cook, dishwasher if assigned storage duties, receiver, kitchen lead, or manager on duty.
When It Is Performed:
When receiving product, storing product, pulling product, restocking stations, organizing coolers, organizing freezers, or
reviewing inventory.
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Labels
Date stickers
Storage shelves
Food containers
Receiving product
Shelf-life guide
Inventory or par sheets if applicable
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Do not use expired, spoiled, or questionable product.
Keep food at safe temperatures during rotation.
Store raw products safely and below ready-to-eat products according to policy.
Follow health department and company storage requirements.
Step-by-Step Procedure:

1. Check product dates before storing or pulling product.
2. Place older product in front or on top according to storage setup.
3. Place newer product behind or below older product when appropriate.
4. Do not mix old and new product unless allowed by policy.
5. Check for expired, spoiled, damaged, or questionable product.
6. Notify manager of product that should be discarded or reviewed.
7. Keep labels visible.
8. Keep storage areas organized.
9. Rotate product during restocking and line setup.
10. Review rotation during cooler, freezer, and dry storage checks.
Quality Standard:
Product is stored so older usable product is used first, labels are visible, and expired or questionable product is removed
according to policy.
Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Putting new product in front of old product.
Using product without checking date.
Mixing old and new product incorrectly.
Leaving expired product in storage.


Hiding old product behind new product.
Failing to label product.
Ignoring damaged or spoiled product.
Not telling manager about questionable product.
Manager Verification:
Manager or kitchen lead should verify that:
□ Older product is accessible first.
□ Newer product is placed behind older product.
□ Labels are visible.
□ Dates are checked.
□ Expired product is removed.
□ Questionable product is reported.
□ Storage areas remain organized.

SOP Title: Dish Machine Operation SOP

Station / Process: Dish Machine Operation
Purpose:
To operate the dish machine safely, correctly, and according to restaurant standards.
Why It Matters:
The dish machine is critical to clean plateware, glassware, utensils, pans, and tools. If the machine is used incorrectly,
items may not be clean or sanitized, chemicals may run low, water may not be correct, and the restaurant may fail food
safety standards. Proper machine operation protects guests, employees, and health inspection readiness.
Who Performs This Task:
Dishwasher, kitchen lead, manager, or employee trained to operate the dish machine.
When It Is Performed:
Any time dishes, glassware, utensils, pans, or tools are run through the dish machine.

Cleaning, safety, and sanitation standards protect the restaurant every day.
They protect guests.
They protect employees.
They protect food.
They protect the business.
They protect the restaurant’s reputation.

SOP Title: Handwashing SOP
Department: All Departments
Station / Process: Employee Handwashing
Purpose:
To make sure employees wash their hands correctly and at the proper times.
Why It Matters:
Handwashing is one of the most important food safety habits in a restaurant. Poor handwashing can spread illness,
contaminate food, create health inspection problems, and damage guest trust. Clean hands help protect guests,
employees, and the restaurant.
Who Performs This Task:
All employees, including managers, cooks, prep cooks, dishwashers, servers, hosts, bartenders, bussers, food runners,
and trainers.
When It Is Performed:
Before handling food
Before putting on gloves
After using the restroom
After touching raw food
After touching trash
After touching face, hair, phone, clothing, or personal items
After coughing, sneezing, eating, drinking, or wiping nose
After cleaning or using chemicals
After handling dirty dishes
After returning from break
Any time hands may be contaminated
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Approved handwashing sink
Warm water if available according to facility setup
Soap
Paper towels or approved hand dryer
Trash can
Handwashing signage if required
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Handwashing must happen at an approved handwashing sink.
Food prep sinks, dish sinks, and service sinks should not be used for handwashing unless specifically approved by health
department and restaurant policy.
Gloves do not replace handwashing.
Employees must wash hands before putting on new gloves.
Step-by-Step Procedure:

1. Go to the approved handwashing sink.
2. Wet hands according to restaurant procedure.
3. Apply soap.
4. Scrub hands thoroughly, including backs of hands, between fingers, under nails, and wrists.


5. Scrub for the required time according to health department or restaurant policy.
6. Rinse hands completely.
7. Dry hands using approved method.
8. Turn off faucet according to restaurant procedure.
9. Return to work only after hands are clean.
10. Put on gloves after handwashing if gloves are required for the task.
Quality Standard:
Employees wash hands at the correct times, using the correct sink and approved handwashing method.
Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Skipping handwashing because gloves are used.
Washing hands in food prep sinks.
Rushing handwashing.
Not washing after touching phone, face, hair, trash, or raw food.
Returning from restroom without washing hands.
Putting on gloves with dirty hands.
Using sanitizer bucket instead of washing hands.
Manager Verification:
Manager or trainer should verify that:
□ Employee knows when to wash hands.
□ Employee uses approved handwashing sink.
□ Employee follows correct handwashing steps.
□ Employee washes hands before gloves.
□ Employee understands gloves do not replace handwashing.
□ Employee corrects handwashing mistakes immediately.

Purpose:
To make sure sanitizer buckets are prepared, used, stored, and changed correctly.
Why It Matters:
Sanitizer buckets help employees keep food-contact and work surfaces sanitary during the shift. If sanitizer is too weak, too
strong, dirty, or used incorrectly, it may not protect the restaurant properly and can create food safety or chemical
concerns.
Who Performs This Task:

When It Is Performed:
Before service
Before prep begins
At station setup
During shift
After sanitizer becomes dirty

After spills or contamination
During closing
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Approved sanitizer
Sanitizer bucket
Clean towels
Test strips if required
Water source
Label if required
Timer or change schedule if used
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Use only approved sanitizer.

SOP Title: Three-Compartment Sink SOP
Department: Back of House / Dish / Bar
Station / Process: Manual Washing, Rinsing, and Sanitizing
Purpose:
To make sure items washed manually are cleaned and sanitized correctly using the three-compartment sink process.
Why It Matters:
The three-compartment sink is important when washing items that cannot go through the dish machine or when the
machine is not available. If the sink is used incorrectly, items may look clean but may not be properly sanitized. Correct use
protects food safety, cleanliness, and health inspection readiness.
Who Performs This Task:
Dishwasher, cook, prep cook, bartender, kitchen lead, or trained employee assigned to manual washing.
When It Is Performed:
When washing pots, pans, utensils, bar tools, prep tools, equipment parts, or other items that require manual washing.
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Three-compartment sink
Approved detergent
Approved sanitizer
Test strips if required
Scrub pads or brushes
Drying rack
Gloves if required
Thermometer if required
Drain plugs
Safety or Sanitation Notes:
Follow restaurant and health department requirements.
Use chemicals according to label.
Do not mix chemicals.
Keep water and sanitizer at required levels and conditions.
Air dry items according to policy.
Do not towel-dry unless approved.
Keep dirty and clean items separated.
Step-by-Step Procedure:


1. Clean and set up the sink before use.
2. Fill first compartment for washing according to procedure.
3. Fill second compartment for rinsing according to procedure.
4. Fill third compartment for sanitizing according to procedure.
5. Test sanitizer strength if required.
6. Scrape food from items before washing.
7. Wash items in first compartment.
8. Rinse items in second compartment.
9. Sanitize items in third compartment for required contact time.
10. Place items on clean drying rack.
11. Allow items to air dry according to policy.
12. Store items only after they are clean and dry.
13. Change water or sanitizer when dirty, too weak, too strong, or no longer acceptable.
Quality Standard:
Items are washed, rinsed, sanitized, air dried, and stored properly using the approved three-compartment sink process.
Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Skipping rinse step.
Skipping sanitizer step.
Not testing sanitizer when required.
Using dirty water.
Mixing wash, rinse, and sanitizer steps.
Towel-drying when not allowed.
Stacking wet items.
Putting clean items on dirty surfaces.
Using sink for handwashing or food prep when not approved.
Manager Verification:
Manager or trainer should verify that:
□ Employee sets up sink correctly.
□ Employee knows wash, rinse, sanitize order.
□ Employee tests sanitizer if required.
□ Employee follows contact time if required.
□ Employee air dries items correctly.
□ Employee changes water and sanitizer when needed.
□ Employee separates dirty and clean items.

SOP Title: Food Temperature Check SOP
Department: Back of House / Management
Station / Process: Food Temperature Monitoring
Purpose:
To make sure food temperatures are checked, recorded, corrected, and verified according to restaurant policy.
Why It Matters:
Temperature control protects food safety. Food held, cooked, cooled, reheated, or stored at unsafe temperatures can
create serious risk. Clear temperature checks help managers catch problems before they become unsafe or costly.
Who Performs This Task:
Cook, prep cook, kitchen lead, manager, or certified food safety employee if required.
When It Is Performed:
During opening checks, cooking, hot holding, cold holding, cooling, reheating, receiving, and manager walkthroughs.
Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Food thermometer
Temperature log
Sanitizer or alcohol wipes for thermometer
Pen

Safety or Sanitation Notes:

Step-by-Step Procedure:

3. Insert thermometer properly according to training.
4. Wait for accurate reading.
5. Record temperature if required.
6. Compare reading to restaurant standard.
7. Take corrective action if temperature is outside standard.
8. Notify manager of unsafe or questionable temperatures.
9. Recheck after correction if required.
10. Clean and store thermometer properly.
Quality Standard:
Temperatures are checked accurately, recorded when required, corrected when needed, and verified by management.
Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Using an unclean thermometer.
Guessing by touch.
Failing to record temperatures.
Ignoring unsafe readings.
Not notifying manager.
Not rechecking after correction.
Using the wrong thermometer method.
Leaving thermometer dirty.

7. Evacuate to designated meeting area if needed.
8. Do not allow employees to collect personal items if unsafe.
9. Account for employees as much as possible.
10. Wait for emergency personnel or approved authority.
11. Do not re-enter until cleared.
12. Document incident after the situation is safe.
Quality Standard:

When It Is Performed:

Tools / Equipment / Supplies Needed:
Thermometer

Incident Form

Keep doors closed.

Step-by-Step Procedure:

7. Contact maintenance or ownership according to policy.

Phone

Previous Version 1.0 Training Required Yes

SOP Title: Host Stand Opening
Purpose: Make sure the host stand, entrance, reservation information, menus, seating chart, and guest waiting area are ready
before the first guest arrives.
Responsibility: Opening host completes the procedure. Opening manager verifies readiness.
Required Supplies: Clean menus, pens, reservation or waitlist system, seating chart, table map, high-chair and booster-seat
access, current specials, and manager communication method.
Procedure
1. Arrive at the host stand 20 minutes before opening.
2. Remove trash, old notes, personal items, and unnecessary paper.
3. Clean the host stand, phone, menus, menu holders, and guest-facing surfaces.
4. Confirm the reservation and waitlist system is operational.
5. Review reservations, large parties, accessibility notes, and expected arrival times.
6. Review the table map and server sections with the opening manager.
7. Confirm menus, children’s menus, and current feature information are ready.
8. Inspect the entrance and waiting area for cleanliness, safety, and clear walkways.
9. Confirm high chairs and booster seats are clean and accessible.
10. Report missing supplies, system problems, seating concerns, or unresolved guest notes before opening.
Critical Standard: The host stand is clean, stocked, organized, and fully informed before doors open. The host can explain sections,
reservations, and escalation needs without guessing.
Manager Verification: Opening manager inspects the station, asks the host to explain reservations and sections, confirms systems
are operational, and signs the checklist before opening.
Change Summary for Version 2.0: Added reservation review, entrance safety check, and manager verification before opening.

GLOSSARY OF SOP SYSTEM TERMS
Working definitions used in this workbook. Apply restaurant policy and controlling legal, safety, regulatory, insurance, or company
requirements when they differ from a template or internal definition.

Approved Version - The current SOP version that has completed the restaurant's required review and approval process. Only
the approved current version should be posted, trained, or used as the operating standard.
Approver - The person with final authority to release an SOP for use. The approver assigns or confirms the effective date and
must match the restaurant's approved authority structure.
Audit Trail - A record showing who created, changed, approved, accessed, replaced, or retired a controlled document when the
restaurant's system requires that history.
Checklist - A short shift-use tool that helps employees or managers confirm that repeated work was completed. A checklist
supports an SOP; it does not replace the full procedure used for training.
Common Mistakes to Avoid - The predictable errors or weak habits an SOP identifies so employees and managers can prevent
repeat problems before they become normal practice.
Controlled Copy - An approved copy of an SOP placed in a binder, station, training packet, or approved digital location. When
the SOP changes, outdated controlled copies must be removed or clearly retired.
Corrective Action - The documented response to a failure, missed standard, incident, or repeated problem. It records what was
corrected, who owns the follow-up, and when the result will be checked again.
Critical Failure - A readiness-assessment failure involving a material issue such as safety, document control, or unauthorized
approval. A critical failure can prevent a manager from being marked ready even when the numerical score is otherwise high.
Critical Standard - A requirement that must be protected because failure can materially affect safety, legality, food quality, cost,
guest experience, security, or operational control.
Customization - The process of adapting a model SOP or template to the restaurant's actual building, equipment, menu, staffing,
technology, policies, authority structure, and applicable requirements before training or release.


GLOSSARY OF SOP SYSTEM TERMS - CONTINUED
Department Owner - The manager or approved leader responsible for maintaining the SOP within the department and
coordinating review, training, revision, and follow-up under the restaurant's authority structure.
Document Control - The system for identifying the current SOP, its version, owner, approver, effective date, change history,
posting locations, and review date so conflicting or outdated standards are not used.
Effective Date - The date an approved SOP officially becomes the restaurant's current operating standard.
Emergency SOP - A written response template for an emergency or serious disruption. It must be customized to the restaurant
and does not replace law, emergency services, official guidance, insurance requirements, or approved company policy.
Form - A documentation tool used to record what happened, who was involved, what action was taken, what follow-up is required,
who owns that follow-up, and the deadline or review date.
Incident Report - A written record of an incident or serious operational event. It should be stored and routed according to the
restaurant's approved records and confidentiality requirements.
Manager Verification - A manager's documented confirmation that the required standard was actually followed. Verification
should be based on observable work, evidence, or real operating conditions rather than assumption.
Master Index - The controlled list used to organize SOPs and track their IDs, titles, versions, owners, status, and review
information. It must be updated when a document is created, revised, replaced, or retired.
Model SOP - A completed example supplied by this Builder to demonstrate the required structure and level of detail. A model
SOP must still be customized and approved before becoming a restaurant standard.
Operational Risk - The exposure created when a process fails or is unclear. In this workbook, risk may include guest safety, food
safety, employee safety, legal or regulatory exposure, financial loss, guest impact, disruption, frequency, or training difficulty.


GLOSSARY OF SOP SYSTEM TERMS - CONTINUED
Previous Version Replaced - The earlier SOP version that the new approved version supersedes. Identifying it helps managers
remove outdated copies and prevent employees from following conflicting instructions.
Quality Standard - The observable description of what correct completion looks like. It gives employees a clear target and gives
managers something specific to verify.
Readiness Assessment - The internal workbook assessment used to determine whether a manager understands and can apply
the SOP system. It is not an outside credential or professional certification.
Readiness Verification - The restaurant's internal confirmation that a manager demonstrated the ability to build, approve, train,
verify, revise, and maintain SOPs under the restaurant's approved authority structure.
Real-Shift Verification - Observation of the standard during actual operating conditions, or an approved operational simulation
when real-shift observation is not practical. Classroom explanation alone is not treated as sufficient proof of performance.
Record Retention - The approved period and method for keeping completed records. This workbook does not establish one
universal retention period; the restaurant must follow applicable company, insurance, payroll, employment, food-safety, and legal
requirements.
Retired SOP - A procedure that is no longer the current standard. Retired copies should be clearly marked, dated, removed from
active use, and stored or disposed of according to the restaurant's records policy.
Review Cycle - The planned rhythm for reviewing an SOP, plus earlier review after incidents, repeated failures, equipment or
menu changes, policy changes, or other material operational changes.
Review Date - The scheduled date by which an SOP should be reviewed for accuracy, relevance, compliance, and operational fit.
Risk-Priority Matrix - The workbook tool that scores safety, legal, financial, guest, operational, frequency, and training exposure
so management can decide whether to create, revise, retrain, increase verification, or retire a procedure.


GLOSSARY OF SOP SYSTEM TERMS - CONTINUED
SOP - Standard Operating Procedure. A written standard for how a task, station, process, or situation should be handled so it can
be trained, followed, verified, reviewed, and updated.
SOP ID - The unique identification number or code assigned to a controlled SOP so managers can distinguish, index, revise, and
track it accurately.
Station Poster - A short visual reminder of the most important behaviors, safety points, or steps from an approved SOP. It is a
quick-reference tool, not a replacement for the full SOP.
Supporting Tool - A poster, checklist, form, log, sign-off page, or verification tool created to make an SOP usable during real
operations and to produce the evidence needed for follow-up.
Training Sign-Off - The documented confirmation that an employee received training and demonstrated the required standard.
Sign-off should follow explanation, demonstration, practice, observation, correction, and verification.
Truth -> Cost -> System - The Gravity decision sequence used in this Builder: identify what is actually failing, expose the
operational cost or risk, then choose the smallest useful system response - build, revise, retrain, verify, or retire.
Version - The identifier assigned to a particular approved edition of an SOP. A version changes when an approved revision
changes the controlled standard.
Version Control - The practice of keeping one clearly identified current version in use while removing or retiring superseded
versions from stations, binders, digital folders, training packets, and other controlled locations.
Why It Matters - The required SOP section that connects a task to real restaurant outcomes such as safety, cost, food quality,
guest experience, speed, consistency, cleanliness, or accountability.

79. Restaurant SOP Binder Setup Guide
Purpose:
To organize all restaurant SOPs, posters, checklists, forms, sign-off pages, and review logs into one clear system.
Why It Matters:
An SOP system becomes weak when documents are scattered, outdated, missing, or hard to find. A well-organized binder
helps managers train employees, verify standards, update procedures, and hold the team accountable.
Recommended Binder Sections:

1. SOP Master Index
2. Front of House SOPs
3. Bar SOPs
4. Back of House Cook Line SOPs
5. Prep SOPs
6. Dish Pit SOPs
7. Cleaning / Safety / Sanitation SOPs
8. Opening and Closing SOPs
9. Manager Process SOPs
10. Emergency SOPs
11. Station Posters
12. Opening and Closing Checklists
13. Manager Verification Forms
14. Training Sign-Off Pages
15. Incident and Corrective Action Forms
16. SOP Review and Update Logs
17. Retired or Replaced SOPs
Binder Setup Steps:

1. Choose a binder, digital folder, or both.
2. Create a master index.
3. Number or code each SOP.
4. Place SOPs in department order.
5. Add checklists behind the SOPs they support.
6. Add forms behind the process they document.
7. Add training sign-off pages in a training section.
8. Add station posters in a poster section.
9. Mark emergency SOPs clearly.
10. Add review dates to each SOP.
11. Assign one manager to maintain the binder.
12. Review binder monthly or according to restaurant policy.
Binder Standard:


The SOP binder should be easy for a manager, trainer, or owner to open and find the correct standard quickly.
Common Mistakes to Avoid:
Keeping SOPs in random folders.
Not numbering SOPs.
Letting outdated SOPs stay active.
Posting standards that do not match the binder.
Not adding review dates.
Not assigning binder ownership.
Creating SOPs but never training them.

Recommended Rollout Order:

3. Station setup SOPs
4. Guest service SOPs
5. Food quality and portion control SOPs
6. Manager process SOPs
7. Emergency SOPs
8. Custom station SOPs
Training Rollout Steps:

1. Choose the first 5 to 10 SOPs to train.
2. Assign a trainer or manager to each SOP.
3. Schedule training by position.
4. Explain why each SOP matters.
5. Demonstrate the task.
6. Let employees practice.
7. Observe employees during real shift conditions.
8. Correct mistakes.
9. Sign off only when performance is ready.
10. Schedule follow-up review.
