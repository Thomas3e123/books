How to Use This Working Book
This book is the recurring inventory-and-purchasing companion to Restaurant Bar, Beverage & Pour Cost Control. The
first book establishes what the beverage program should be. This book records what the restaurant actually counts,
targets, orders, receives, pays for, reviews, and changes.
The operating cycle is:
COUNT → COMPARE TO TARGET → CONSIDER EXPECTED DEMAND → BUILD ORDER → MANAGER REVIEW
→ ORDER → RECEIVE → VERIFY → REVIEW WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED
Do not order from memory. Do not order from an empty shelf. Order from inventory, demand, par, delivery timing,
and a management decision.
• Set up the master inventory once, then maintain it.
• Use category-specific count/order forms at the frequency the operation requires.
• Use pars for normal demand and Temporary Targets for approved exceptions.
• Document management adjustments instead of hiding them inside the order quantity.
• Separate beverage-product purchases from bar-supply purchases.
• Use the weekly record to preserve the operating story without repeating the largest master sheets 52 times.
• Use monthly and annual reviews to decide what should change.

PART I - BUILD A RELIABLE INVENTORY BASELINE
Chapter 1 - Inventory Is Money in Another Form
A beverage shelf is a financial record. Liquor, wine, beer, mixers, syrups, garnishes, and supplies represent cash that has
been converted into operating inventory. The purpose of counting is not to admire what is on the shelf. It is to know what
the restaurant owns, what is usable, what it is worth, and what decision should follow.
The first rule of this book is simple:
Physical inventory is not automatically usable inventory.
A damaged bottle, spoiled juice, expired open wine, product committed to an event, or item already designated for
disposal may be physically present while being unavailable for normal ordering decisions.
Usable On Hand = Physical On Hand - Unusable / Committed Product

Chapter 2 - Count the Same Thing the Same Way
Reliable ordering begins with a repeatable count. Use the same count path, unit definitions, package sizes, and open-
bottle method. A change in counting method can create apparent variance even when the operation did not change.
• Count in shelf order, not memory order.
• Define the purchase unit and count unit.
• Use one approved open-bottle method.
• Separate full, open, damaged, committed, and unusable quantities when they affect the decision.
• Have management verify high-risk or unusual counts.

Riverside Grill is the fictional example restaurant used throughout this book. It is a full-service restaurant with a moderate
bar, lunch and dinner, weekend volume, standard cocktails, wine by the glass, packaged and draft beer, and a small zero-
proof program.
Its normal weekly cycle is Sunday inventory, Monday order, Tuesday primary delivery, and Friday secondary delivery
where needed. This week it also has a Saturday private event for approximately 80 additional guests featuring Margaritas,
Palomas, house wine, domestic beer, and zero-proof grapefruit coolers.

PART II - PARS, TARGETS & DEMAND
Chapter 6 - What a Par Actually Means
Par = the approved normal inventory level.
A par should reflect expected usage, time until reliable replenishment, reasonable safety allowance, known demand
patterns, and storage reality. It is not “what we have always kept.”
A useful operating thought process is: expected usage + time until reliable replenishment + reasonable safety allowance +
known demand adjustments, converted into practical purchase units.
Product              Current Par              Avg Weekly Use           Peak Use                Review Decision
House Vodka          5                        3.1                      4.0                     Keep 5
Gin                  4                        1.7                      2.5                     Reduce to 3
Bourbon              6                        4.2                      5.1                     Keep 6
Blanco Tequila       6                        4.5                      5.8                     Increase to 7
Light Rum            4                        1.9                      2.8                     Keep 4
Campari              2                        0.6                      1.0                     Keep 2

Chapter 7 - Temporary Target Is Not a New Par
Temporary Target = a temporary event or exception inventory level.
Target Need = the applicable level used for the current order. Under normal conditions Target Need is the approved Par.
During an approved event or exception, Target Need may be the Temporary Target.
For Riverside Grill’s Saturday event, Blanco Tequila has a normal par of 6, 3.3 usable bottles on hand, and an approved
Temporary Target of 8. The mathematical need is 4.7 bottles; the final order is 5 bottles. After the event, the Temporary
Target expires. The normal par does not become 8 unless later evidence supports a permanent change.

Chapter 8 - Event Demand Changes More Than the Obvious Bottle
A Cinco de Mayo-style example illustrates the same rule. Expected covers rise from about 210 to 325, an increase of 115.
Temporary targets may change across the full beverage architecture:
Item                 Normal Target            Event Target             Usable On Hand          Suggested Order
Blanco Tequila       7                        12                       4.2                     8
Orange Liqueur       3                        5                        2.1                     3
Grapefruit           5                        10                       4                       6
Agave                4                        8                        3                       5
Lime Prep            6                        12                       5                       7
Ginger Beer          48                       72                       39                      33
Temporary targets expire after the event. Specialty inventory is reviewed. No automatic reorder occurs merely because an
event consumed product.

Chapter 9 - Par Changes Require Evidence
• Repeated stockouts under normal demand
• Repeated excess inventory with no operational reason
• Reliable change in delivery schedule
• Permanent menu change
• Documented sustained volume change
• Storage or cash constraints
• New substitute or product rationalization
A par change is a management decision. Document the evidence, effective date, owner, and review date.

PART III - GRAVITY SMART ORDER
Chapter 10 - Build the Order from the Target
Suggested Order = Target Need - Usable Inventory On Hand
Final Order = Suggested Order +/- Approved Management Adjustment
The adjustment is not hidden. It requires a reason. That preserves the difference between what the math suggested and
what management decided.
Light Rum example: par 4, usable on hand 3.6, suggested need 0.4. Final order: 0 bottles. A fractional need does not
automatically justify purchasing another full bottle.
Sweet Vermouth example: par 2, usable on hand 1.5, suggested need 0.5. Final order: 0 bottles when delivery timing and
expected demand support holding.
Blanco Tequila event example: Temporary Target 8, usable on hand 3.3, suggested need 4.7. Final order: 5 bottles.

Chapter 11 - Approved Order Exceptions
Management adjustments should use a reason that can be understood later. Approved exception categories include:
• Event
• Holiday
• Weather
• Large Reservation
• Promotion
• Delivery Change
• Vendor Shortage
• Price Change
• Menu Change
• Stockout Recovery
• Seasonal Transition
• Other documented reason

Chapter 13 - The Empty-Shelf Trap
An empty shelf is not automatically instruction to buy. First ask whether the product still belongs in the program. A
specialty melon liqueur with 1.4 bottles remaining, zero permanent drinks, Menu Reach of 0, and a Deplete status should
not be reordered merely because the last bottle eventually becomes empty.
The purpose of depletion is to stop dead inventory from recreating itself.

PART IV - RECEIVING & COST CHANGES
Chapter 14 - Receiving Is the Second Half of Ordering
An order is not complete when it is submitted. The restaurant must verify what actually arrives. Receiving should compare
ordered quantity, delivered quantity, expected unit cost, invoice cost, substitutions, damage, shortages, and credits due.

Chapter 15 - Cost Changes Must Travel
Riverside Grill expected Bourbon at $28.00 per bottle. The invoice shows $29.50. The dollar change is $1.50 and the cost
change is approximately 5.36%. That information should not stop at the invoice. It should reach the cost-change register
and any recipe or pricing review that depends on the product.
Dollar Change = New Cost - Previous Cost
Cost Change % = Dollar Change ÷ Previous Cost × 100

Chapter 16 - Shortage Is a Management Decision
If 5 bottles of tequila were ordered and only 4 were delivered, management decides what happens next: accept partial
delivery, source a legal/approved substitute, reduce the event target, place an approved secondary order, or change the
offer. Do not allow the shortage to become an undocumented improvisation.

Chapter 17 - Credits Must Close the Loop
Damage, shortages, invoice corrections, and approved returns are not finished when somebody says, “The vendor will
credit us.” Track the amount, vendor, invoice, reason, expected credit date, actual credit date, and person who verified the
credit.

PART V - VENDORS & PURCHASING
Chapter 18 - Cheapest Is Not Automatically Best
Vendor comparison should include price, package size, order minimums, delivery frequency, emergency support, fill rate,
shortages, substitution practices, credit handling, product availability, quality, and administrative burden.
Factor                                  Vendor A                                 Vendor B
Example case price                      $118 / 12 = $9.83                        $112 / 12 = $9.33
Minimum                                 $500                                     $750
Delivery                                3x weekly                                2x weekly
Emergency support                       Yes                                      No
Recent shortages                        1                                        4
Credit handling                         Strong                                   Moderate
Vendor B is cheaper by unit in this example. That does not automatically make Vendor B the stronger operating choice.

Chapter 19 - Separate Product Purchases from Operating Supplies
Beverage Product Purchases are product costs that can enter beverage-cost calculations. Operating Supply Purchases-
such as napkins, straws, bar towels, disposable items, or cleaning/support supplies-do not belong inside beverage product
cost simply because they were ordered by the bar.
Mixing the categories corrupts cost analysis.

PART VI - ACCURACY, VARIANCE & DEAD INVENTORY
Chapter 21 - Inventory Accuracy Test
Previous Count + Purchases + Transfers In - Transfers Out - Documented Usage / Adjustments = Expected
Ending Inventory
Example: 8 + 6 - 1 - 7 = 6 expected units. Physical ending inventory is 5.2. Difference: 0.8. Investigate the difference
before assigning a cause.

Chapter 22 - Actual Product Usage at Cost
Actual Product Usage at Cost = Beginning Inventory + Purchases + Transfers In - Transfers Out - Ending
Inventory
This is a financial calculation using dollar values. Documented adjustments remain separate according to the restaurant’s
accounting structure. Do not mix this dollar calculation with physical-unit usage.
Monthly Riverside Grill example: Beginning Inventory $8,420 + Purchases $7,860 + Transfers In $120 - Transfers Out $75
- Ending Inventory $8,615 = Actual Product Usage at Cost $7,710.
Beverage Sales: $31,850. Actual Beverage Cost %: 24.21%. Theoretical Product Cost: $7,420, or 23.30%. Difference:
$290. The $290 difference is not automatically theft. It is an investigation target.

Chapter 23 - Dead Inventory Tells the Truth
Product                  Value                    Menu Reach                Last Meaningful Use      Status
Melon Liqueur            $27                      0                         83 days                  Deplete
Specialty Rum            $62                      1                         41 days                  Review
Premium Gin              $108                     2                         8 days                   Keep
Seasonal Syrup           $32                      0                         21 days                  Seasonal Hold
Dead inventory is not solved by irresponsible promotion. Use approved features, depletion, legal returns where available,
product substitution/rationalization, or a documented retirement decision consistent with responsible service and
applicable law.

Chapter 24 - Count Accuracy Before Behavior Conclusions
When actual usage and theoretical usage differ, first verify the count, units, purchases, transfers, recipe version, POS
mapping, documented waste, and portion standard. A variance is a question, not an accusation.

APPENDIX A - GLOSSARY
Actual Beverage Cost %: Actual Product Usage at Cost divided by Beverage Sales × 100, using the restaurant’s defined
reporting categories.
Actual Product Usage at Cost: Dollar-based financial usage: Beginning Inventory + Purchases + Transfers In -
Transfers Out - Ending Inventory.
Actual Usage: Physical product movement calculated in consistent physical units. Do not mix with dollar-based usage.
Dead Inventory: Product consuming cash and space without sufficient useful movement or approved purpose.
Deplete - D: Status instructing the operation to use responsibly where approved but not automatically reorder.
Extended Cost: Purchase Quantity × Unit Cost.
Final Order: Suggested Order plus or minus an approved management adjustment.
Gravity Smart Order: The controlled order process that starts with Target Need and Usable Inventory On Hand, then
documents management adjustment.
Inventory Value: Quantity On Hand × Unit Cost.
Menu Reach: Number of approved, sellable drinks an ingredient helps the restaurant produce.
Operating Supply Purchase: Bar operating supplies that do not enter beverage-product cost calculations.
Par: Approved normal inventory level.
Physical On Hand: Product physically present before unusable or committed product is removed from the ordering
decision.
Suggested Order: Target Need - Usable Inventory On Hand.
Target Need: The applicable target used for the current order: normally Par, or an approved Temporary Target during an
exception.
Temporary Target: Approved temporary inventory level for an event, holiday, shortage recovery, or other documented
exception.
Unit Cost: Cost of one defined purchase/count unit.
Usable Inventory On Hand: Physical On Hand minus unusable or committed product.
Usage Variance: Actual Usage - Theoretical Usage. Positive means actual usage exceeded theoretical usage. Variance
is a question, not an accusation.
Vendor Credit: Documented amount owed back to the restaurant for an approved shortage, damage, return, pricing
correction, or other valid reason.


APPENDIX B - FORMULA QUICK REFERENCE
Inventory Value = Quantity On Hand × Unit Cost
Open-Bottle Value = Open-Bottle Equivalent × Full-Bottle Unit Cost
Extended Cost = Purchase Quantity × Unit Cost
Usable Inventory On Hand = Physical On Hand - Unusable / Committed Product
Suggested Order = Target Need - Usable Inventory On Hand
Final Order = Suggested Order +/- Approved Management Adjustment
Target Need = Par under normal conditions / Temporary Target during an approved exception
Actual Product Usage at Cost = Beginning Inventory + Purchases + Transfers In - Transfers Out - Ending
Inventory
Physical Actual Usage = Beginning Quantity + Purchases + Transfers In - Transfers Out - Ending Quantity
Usage Variance = Actual Usage - Theoretical Usage
Expected Ending Inventory = Previous Count + Purchases + Transfers In - Transfers Out - Documented Usage /
Adjustments
Dollar Change = New Cost - Previous Cost
Cost Change % = Dollar Change ÷ Previous Cost × 100
Beverage Cost % = Beverage Cost ÷ Beverage Sales × 100
Truth first. Calculation second. Decision third. Action fourth. Verification always.
A formula never replaces judgment. Check the count, units, purchases, transfers, recipes, POS records, waste, receiving,
and operating standards before assigning a cause.

RESPONSIBILITY & VERIFICATION STANDARD
Every recurring task in this book should answer four questions: Who owns the task? Who verifies it? When is it due? What
happens when it is wrong?
Use role or function before job title where practical. Verification should be risk-based. Small restaurants may use
compensating verification when full segregation of duties is not possible.
The system owns the process. A person owns the task. Management owns the exception. Leadership owns the
unresolved risk. No important operating problem should survive simply because everyone assumed somebody
else was handling it.
