Introduction

The Bar Is Not Profitable Just Because It Is Busy

A busy bar can hide a lot of mistakes.

A bartender can pour drinks all night. Servers can sell cocktails at every table. The register can show thousands of dollars
in beverage sales. Guests can be happy, and management can walk away believing the bar had a great night.

And it still may have underperformed.

A little extra liquor in every pour does not look like much.

A bottle ordered because somebody might need it does not look dangerous.

Three limes thrown away at closing do not look important.

A complimentary drink that was never recorded does not feel like theft.

A cocktail made differently by four bartenders may still taste good enough that nobody complains.

A bottle sitting untouched on the back bar for nine months does not create an alarm.

That is exactly why beverage operations can become expensive without looking broken.

The losses are often small enough individually to escape attention and frequent enough collectively to matter.

Gravity begins with a different question.

Not:

“How much did the bar sell?”

But:

“What should the bar have produced from what we purchased, and what happened to the difference?”

That question changes the way a manager sees the bar.




The Purpose of This Workbook

This is not simply a cocktail recipe book.

It is not simply an inventory book.

And it is not a collection of forms designed to give managers more paperwork.

And what should we change?

If those questions cannot be answered, the bar is being managed largely by habit.

Habit is not a control system.




One Bottle Should Have More Than One Job

One of the principles used throughout this workbook is simple:

Shelf space must earn its place.

Restaurants frequently build beverage inventories backward.

Someone finds an interesting cocktail and buys a specialty bottle for it.

Another recipe requires another bottle.

A bartender recommends something else.
THE GRAVITY SYSTEM                                                                                            5

A distributor has a promotion.

A seasonal drink gets removed from the menu, but its ingredients remain.

Eventually, the restaurant has hundreds or thousands of dollars tied up in bottles that contribute very little to sales.

We will approach inventory differently.

First, we will establish what the restaurant needs to be capable of making.

Then we will determine the smallest practical inventory that provides that capability.

Every major ingredient will be connected to the drinks it enables.

We will call this Menu Reach.

Menu Reach = The number of approved, sellable drinks an ingredient helps the restaurant produce.

A bottle used in fifteen popular drinks has a different operational value from a bottle used in one drink that sells twice a
month.

That does not mean specialty products are bad.

It means specialty products should have to justify themselves.




Three Levels of Bar Capability

This workbook divides inventory into three practical levels.


Essential Bar

The Essential Bar contains the ingredients necessary to produce the major drinks guests reasonably expect from a full-
service restaurant.

It is deliberately controlled.

The objective is not to have the biggest back bar.

The objective is to achieve the greatest useful drink capability from the smallest responsible inventory.


Full-Service Bar

The Full-Service Bar adds products that significantly expand cocktail selection, guest choice and selling opportunities
without creating unnecessary inventory complexity.

These additions should have sufficient Menu Reach or sales potential to justify carrying them.

6                                                                                                         THE GRAVITY SYSTEM

Expanded Bar

The Expanded Bar contains specialty spirits, liqueurs, modifiers and concept-specific products.

This is where experimentation belongs.

It is also where discipline matters most.

An Expanded Bar should grow because demand and concept justify it—not because bottles slowly accumulated.




The Capability Question

Throughout this workbook, managers will repeatedly ask:

What can we make with what we already have?

That question becomes especially valuable when designing seasonal menus.

A Valentine's cocktail does not automatically require three new bottles.

A Christmas menu does not need to leave six specialty products gathering dust in January.

A summer feature does not need to complicate the entire inventory system.

Before purchasing another ingredient, we will first determine what can be built from the restaurant's existing inventory.

Then we ask:

If we add one ingredient, how much additional menu capability does it create?

That is a better purchasing question.




The Standard Recipe Principle

There is another truth a profitable beverage program cannot avoid:

If a drink does not have a standard recipe, it does not have a reliable cost.

If one bartender pours 1½ ounces and another pours 2 ounces, those are financially different products even if the menu
gives them the same name and price.

If garnish, mixer, glassware or build method changes depending on who is working, the restaurant does not really have a
recipe.

A bartender can contribute ideas.

The Gravity Beverage Standard

The objective of this workbook can be reduced to five expectations:

Know what you have.

Know what it should produce.

Know what you sold.

Know what happened to the difference.

Do something when the numbers stop making sense.

That is where beverage control begins.

Not with suspicion.

Not with a larger stack of paperwork.

With visibility.

Because a restaurant eventually pays for every truth its leaders do not face.

And the bar is no exception.




CHAPTER 1

Where Beverage Profit Actually Goes

The most dangerous beverage losses are not always dramatic.

A missing case of liquor gets attention.

A half-ounce overpour repeated hundreds of times often does not.

That distinction matters.

Consider a bartender who routinely pours only one-quarter ounce more than the approved recipe.

One drink barely changes.

One shift may not appear alarming.


THE GRAVITY SYSTEM                                                                               9

But multiply that extra quarter-ounce across every affected drink, every bartender, every week and every month.

The restaurant is not merely losing liquor.

It is selling product without receiving payment for it.

That is different from saying the bartender is stealing.

There are many reasons variance occurs:

poor training, inconsistent measuring, speed, habit, recipe confusion, incorrect glassware, unrecorded comps, spills,
breakage, incorrect ringing, bad inventory counts, receiving mistakes, transfers, waste, or deliberate loss.

Gravity does not begin by deciding which explanation is true.

It begins by making the difference visible.

Then management investigates the truth.

That distinction—evidence before accusation—will remain important throughout this workbook.




The Quarter-Ounce Problem

Suppose a restaurant's standard pour is 1.5 ounces.

A 750 mL bottle contains approximately 25.36 fluid ounces.

At a perfect 1.5-ounce pour, that bottle theoretically provides:

25.36 ÷ 1.5 = approximately 16.9 pours

For practical operating purposes, management may establish a realistic expected yield rather than pretending every
theoretical fraction will become a sale.

Now suppose the bartender routinely pours 1.75 ounces instead.

25.36 ÷ 1.75 = approximately 14.5 pours

The difference is more than two potential drinks from a single bottle.

That is where an apparently insignificant overpour becomes a management issue.

If the restaurant sells that drink for $10, those lost portions represent potential selling opportunities that disappeared
without appearing as an obvious expense on the shift report.

Multiply the behavior across multiple bottles, multiple bartenders and an entire year.

Small errors become large numbers because restaurants repeat small actions thousands of times.

That is one of the central ideas behind Gravity:

Pour Cost Is Not the Same as Beverage Cost

Managers often use several terms interchangeably when they should not.

Throughout this workbook, we will distinguish between them.


Recipe Cost

The cost of all ingredients used to produce one drink.


Beverage Cost Percentage

The cost of the beverage product compared with the revenue generated from selling it.

A simplified formula is:

Beverage Cost % = Beverage Cost ÷ Beverage Sales × 100


Pour Cost

Often used to describe liquor cost as a percentage of liquor sales, although restaurants may define the reporting category
differently.


Contribution Dollars

The dollars remaining after the direct recipe cost is removed from the selling price.

Contribution Dollars = Selling Price − Recipe Cost

A lower cost percentage is not automatically the best decision.

A drink that costs $2 and sells for $10 produces:

$8 contribution

A drink that costs $3 and sells for $14 produces:

$11 contribution

The second drink has a higher recipe cost, but it also contributes more dollars.

Managers need both pieces of information.

Percentage tells one story.

Dollars tell another.

Gravity wants the whole truth.




Theoretical Cost Versus Actual Cost

This distinction is one of the most important concepts in beverage management.

Theoretical cost asks:

Based on exactly what we sold and the approved recipes for those drinks, what should the product have cost?

Actual product usage at cost asks:

Based on the movement and value of our inventory, what product cost did we actually use?

The difference between those numbers deserves attention.
Keep this financial comparison separate from physical Usage Variance. Dollar values explain cost performance; physical
units explain product movement.

Suppose the POS shows that the restaurant sold a particular cocktail 100 times.

The approved recipe calls for 1.5 ounces of a particular spirit.

Theoretically, those drinks should have consumed:

100 × 1.5 ounces = 150 ounces

If inventory movement indicates that substantially more product disappeared, management has a variance.

That variance does not tell management why.

It tells management where to look.

This is an important boundary.

A control system should create visibility.

It should not create accusations.

Variance Is a Question

When the numbers do not match, managers sometimes jump directly to the most emotionally satisfying explanation.

Someone is stealing.

The bartender is overpouring.

Inventory was counted wrong.

The POS is wrong.

The distributor shorted us.

Maybe.

But Gravity does not reward guessing.

A variance is a question that requires investigation.

Possible causes include:

incorrect beginning inventory,
incorrect ending inventory,
receiving errors,
products entered under the wrong item,
transfers that were not recorded,
spills,
breakage,
complimentary beverages,
incorrect recipes,
inconsistent portions,
incorrect POS buttons,
substitutions,
training drinks,
promotional drinks,
unrecorded waste,
counting unit errors,
theft,
or a combination of several small problems.

A manager's job is not to pick the explanation he or she likes.

The manager's job is to find the explanation supported by evidence.

The Four Places Beverage Profit Disappears

Most beverage-control problems eventually connect to four areas.


1. Purchasing

The restaurant buys too much, buys the wrong products, accepts incorrect deliveries, pays prices nobody reviews, or
accumulates products with little sales justification.


2. Storage

Products are poorly secured, poorly organized, improperly rotated, counted inconsistently or allowed to become dead
inventory.


3. Production

Drinks are overpoured, recipes vary, ingredients are wasted, garnishes are uncontrolled, batches are inaccurate or
bartenders substitute products without recording the change.


4. Sales and Recording

Drinks are rung incorrectly, discounts and comps are poorly controlled, items are given away, modifiers are missed, or the
POS does not accurately represent what was produced.

Notice what is missing from this list:

“The bartender.”

People matter, but a good control system does not begin by making one position responsible for every failure.

Management owns the system surrounding the people.

If the recipe is unclear, that is a management problem.

If nobody verifies inventory, that is a management problem.

If five bartenders have five versions of the same drink, that is a management problem.

If employees have never been trained on what constitutes a spill, comp or waste entry, that is a management problem.

Accountability begins with clear expectations.




CHAPTER 2

Every Bottle Needs a Reason to Be There

THE GRAVITY SYSTEM                                                                                                      15

Walk behind enough restaurant bars and you will eventually find the bottle nobody can explain.

It may have been purchased for a cocktail that disappeared from the menu two years ago.

A former bartender may have requested it.

A distributor may have offered a deal.

Someone may have thought guests would order it.

Perhaps they did—for three weeks.

Now it sits.

The bottle is not costing the restaurant more money every day it remains unopened.

But the money already spent on it is trapped.

The shelf space is occupied.

The inventory count is larger.

The operation is more complicated.

And if enough bottles accumulate this way, the restaurant begins maintaining a museum instead of an inventory.

This workbook uses a different rule:

Every permanent inventory item should have an operational reason to remain permanent.




Menu Reach

One of the measurements we will use is Menu Reach.

Menu Reach = The number of approved, sellable drinks an ingredient helps the restaurant produce.

Menu Reach does not determine everything.

A bottle used for one extremely popular and profitable drink may be more valuable than a bottle appearing in eight drinks
nobody orders.

But Menu Reach gives management an immediate question to ask.

Why are we carrying this?

Consider two specialty liqueurs.

Bottle A

Inventory Justification

For management purposes, we can think about inventory justification through three questions:

How many things can we sell with it?

How often are guests likely to buy those things?

How much money do those sales contribute?

A conceptual Gravity measurement is:

Inventory Justification = Menu Reach × Expected Demand × Contribution Value

This is not intended to become a complicated accounting formula.

It is a decision framework.

A restaurant should not buy bottles simply to increase the number of bottles it owns.

The purpose of inventory is to support profitable guest demand.

CHAPTER 3
The Three-Level Bar

There is no single correct inventory for every restaurant.

A neighborhood grill, upscale steakhouse, sports restaurant, seafood restaurant and destination cocktail bar should not
necessarily stock the same products.

But they can use the same decision structure.

This workbook divides bar inventory into three levels.


Level 1 — Essential Bar

The Essential Bar answers:

What is the smallest responsible inventory that allows a full-service restaurant to make the drinks guests commonly
expect?

It prioritizes high-use base spirits, versatile modifiers, standard mixers and ingredients with broad Menu Reach.


Level 2 — Full-Service Bar

The Full-Service Bar expands guest choice and enables additional classics, contemporary restaurant cocktails, aperitifs,
brunch drinks and profitable seasonal features.

Products added at this level should substantially increase capability.


Level 3 — Expanded Bar

The Expanded Bar supports concept-specific drinks, premium selections, lower-volume classics and specialty ingredients.

Expanded does not mean uncontrolled.

It means justified specialization.

The One-More-Ingredient Test

Before adding a specialty ingredient, ask:

What does one more ingredient unlock?

Suppose the current bar can make 45 approved drinks.

Adding one modifier allows the restaurant to make six additional drinks, two of which fit an upcoming seasonal promotion.

That addition may have excellent inventory justification.

Another bottle may unlock only one low-demand cocktail.

The first deserves serious consideration.

The second deserves skepticism.

This simple test will become especially important when we reach the holiday and underused-drink sections.

THE GRAVITY BAR INVENTORY RULE

Before a new permanent bottle is added, management should be able to answer:

1. What will we make with it?

2. Who is likely to buy those drinks?

3. What will those drinks cost?

4. What will we charge?

5. How many existing recipes does it support?

6. Does it replace something we already carry?

7. What happens to the bottle if the feature fails?

If nobody can answer those questions, purchasing the bottle is not a beverage strategy.

It is a guess.

And guesses have a way of becoming inventory.

CHAPTER 5

The Complete Standard On-Hand Inventory

A useful bar inventory is not the largest inventory a restaurant can afford.

It is the smallest practical inventory that allows the restaurant to serve its guests well, execute its menu consistently,
respond to common requests, support profitable features, and maintain enough variety to fit the concept.

That distinction matters.

The objective of this chapter is not to tell every restaurant which brands to buy.

It is to establish capability categories.

A neighborhood restaurant may select value-oriented brands.

A steakhouse may emphasize premium whiskey.

A Mexican restaurant may carry a much deeper tequila selection.

An Italian restaurant may place greater emphasis on wine, aperitifs and digestifs.

The categories can change in depth without abandoning the control system.




THE THREE-TIER INVENTORY MODEL

Every permanent beverage item should be assigned to one of three tiers.


Tier 1 — Essential

Products that support common guest expectations or have substantial Menu Reach.

These form the operating foundation.


Tier 2 — Full-Service

Products that meaningfully expand the restaurant's beverage capability and support additional classics, contemporary
cocktails, brunch, seasonal features or concept needs.


Tier 3 — Expanded

Specialty products, premium depth, concept-specific bottles and lower-frequency ingredients that require a clear business
justification.

If an Expanded product becomes a high-volume ingredient, management may promote it.

If a Full-Service product stops selling, it may be demoted or removed.

The inventory should respond to evidence.

THE GRAVITY PRINCIPLE

A well-designed bar does not need every bottle.

It needs the right relationships between bottles.

When one bottle supports several profitable drinks, inventory becomes productive.

When juices, syrups and garnishes cross multiple recipes, preparation becomes more efficient.

When seasonal drinks reuse core inventory, promotions become less risky.

When managers know what every ingredient can produce, purchasing becomes intentional.

The objective is not minimalism for its own sake.

The objective is capability without unnecessary complexity.

That is the bar we will now use to build the recipe library.

Gravity Baseline Recipe Standard
Cocktail specifications in this book are practical starting standards. They are not claims that one historically correct recipe
exists for every cocktail. A restaurant may establish its own house version. Once the house version is approved, costed,
versioned, programmed, trained, and verified, that house version becomes the restaurant operating standard.


A Recipe Is a Financial Standard

A beverage recipe does more than tell a bartender how to make a drink.

It establishes:

what goes into the drink,
how much goes into the drink,
how the drink is produced,
what glass receives it,
how it is presented,
what the drink should cost,
and what the restaurant has promised the guest.

Change the quantity and the cost changes.

Change the spirit and the cost changes.

Change the garnish and the cost changes.

Change the glass and the guest's perception may change.

That is why the recipe library belongs inside a beverage-control system.

Every restaurant should ultimately approve its own house recipes. The recipes in this library provide practical full-service
starting standards. Where respected variations exist, management should choose the restaurant's approved version and
document it.

Once approved, that version becomes the standard.

CHAPTER 9

Different Does Not Have to Mean Difficult

Restaurants sometimes make one of two mistakes when trying to make their cocktail menu more interesting.

The first is playing it too safe.

The menu becomes a collection of drinks guests already see everywhere, with little reason to choose this restaurant's
beverage program over another.

The second mistake is moving too far in the opposite direction.

The restaurant buys obscure bottles, builds complicated recipes, creates elaborate garnishes, adds slow preparation
steps and gives servers drinks that are difficult to explain.

Neither extreme is necessary.

There is useful territory between ordinary and obscure.

The ten drinks in this chapter were selected because they demonstrate that principle.

They are not here merely because they are less commonly ordered.

They were selected because they can offer some combination of:

recognizable base spirits,
approachable flavors,
manageable preparation,
useful inventory overlap,
strong perceived value,
simple server descriptions,
visual or menu distinction,
and reasonable potential in a full-service restaurant.

They should still be tested.

No book can guarantee what the guests in one particular restaurant will buy.

The purpose is to give management better candidates to test.

CHAPTER 12

Every Ounce Has a Cost

A manager does not need to memorize the cost of every ounce behind the bar.

The manager does need a system capable of producing the answer.

Beverage profitability begins with a simple chain:

Purchase Cost → Usable Quantity → Portion Cost → Recipe Cost → Selling Price → Contribution → Actual
Results

Break any link in that chain and management loses visibility.




KNOW THE PACKAGE

Liquor is commonly purchased in metric bottle sizes while recipes are frequently written in fluid ounces.

For operating calculations, useful approximate conversions include:

Bottle Size                                                   Approximate Fluid Ounces
50 mL                                                         1.69 oz
200 mL                                                        6.76 oz
375 mL                                                        12.68 oz
750 mL                                                        25.36 oz
1L                                                            33.81 oz
1.75 L                                                        59.17 oz

These are mathematical conversions.

They are not promises of perfect usable yield.

Actual operations include normal residue, measurement limitations and other realities.

The purpose of theoretical yield is to establish a standard against which reality can be compared.




COST PER OUNCE

THE GRAVITY SYSTEM                                                                                          117

The basic formula is:

Bottle Cost ÷ Bottle Ounces = Cost Per Ounce

Suppose a 750 mL bottle costs $24.

A 750 mL bottle contains approximately 25.36 fluid ounces.

$24 ÷ 25.36 = approximately $0.95 per ounce

A 1.5-ounce portion therefore costs approximately:

$0.95 × 1.5 = $1.43

A 2-ounce portion costs approximately:

$0.95 × 2 = $1.90

That half-ounce difference matters.

CHAPTER 22

Theoretical Cost Versus Actual Cost

This is where the entire system begins to come together.

Suppose the POS tells us exactly what was sold.

The approved recipes tell us exactly what each sale should have consumed.

Therefore:

Theoretical Usage = Approved Recipe Quantity × Recorded Units Sold, aggregated across all applicable
beverages using the product, in the same physical unit.

Inventory tells us:

Actual Usage = Beginning Inventory + Purchases + Transfers In − Transfers Out − Ending Inventory
Calculate Actual Usage in consistent physical units. Do not mix physical quantities with dollar values. Documented
adjustments remain separate and should be identified under the restaurant’s approved accounting or inventory structure.
Actual Product Usage at Cost = Beginning Inventory Value + Beverage Product Purchases + Transfers In at Cost −
Transfers Out at Cost − Ending Inventory Value
Documented accounting adjustments remain separate according to the restaurant’s accounting structure.

Then:

Usage Variance = Actual Usage − Theoretical Usage
A positive Usage Variance means Actual Usage exceeded Theoretical Usage. Variance is a question requiring
investigation, not an accusation.


130                                                                                                 THE GRAVITY SYSTEM

The precise implementation can vary with the restaurant's accounting and inventory systems.

The management principle does not.

What should have happened?

versus

What actually happened?

That difference deserves explanation.

1. Verify the Data

Was inventory counted correctly?

Were units consistent?

Were transfers handled correctly?


2. Verify the Recipe

Is the POS item connected to the correct recipe?

Has the recipe changed without the costing system being updated?


3. Verify the POS

Were drinks rung under the correct buttons?

Were premium substitutions recorded?

Were comps and promotions entered?


4. Verify Production

Are portions measured?

Are batches correct?

requiring deeper investigation.

A variance does not identify a thief.

BASIC PAR LOGIC

A simple starting concept:
138                                                                                                      THE GRAVITY SYSTEM

CHAPTER 69

More Choices Do Not Automatically Create More Sales

When beverage sales are weak, one of the easiest responses is:

“We need more drinks.”

Maybe.

But more drinks often require more ingredients.

More ingredients create more inventory.

More inventory creates more cash tied up on shelves, more training, more counting, more opportunities for waste and
more products that eventually have to justify their existence.

Before expanding the bar, management should ask a different question:

“Are we fully selling the beverage capability we already own?”

A restaurant with forty available drinks and poor beverage sales may not need fifty drinks.

It may need:

better menu design,

better server knowledge,

better recommendations,

better pricing,

better execution,

and better visibility.

CONCLUSION

The Bottle Is Not the Business

A restaurant can have an impressive backbar and a weak beverage business.

It can have fewer bottles and a very strong one.

The difference is not the number of labels.

It is whether management understands what those labels are supposed to accomplish.

Every bottle represents a decision.

Every recipe represents a standard.

Every pour represents a cost.

Every selling price represents a financial choice.

Every waste entry represents information.

Every variance represents a question.

Every training record represents a promise that someone has been prepared to perform the work.

And every management report represents an opportunity to act before the same problem becomes more expensive.

The goal was never to control bottles for the sake of controlling bottles.

The goal is to build a beverage operation in which the restaurant can explain what is happening.

That means knowing:

REFERENCE & CONTROLLED FORMS

Glossary of Beverage Control Terms
Actual Beverage Cost %: Actual Product Usage at Cost divided by beverage sales, multiplied by 100.
Actual Product Usage at Cost: Beginning inventory value + beverage product purchases + transfers in at cost − transfers
out at cost − ending inventory value. Documented accounting adjustments are identified separately according to the
restaurant’s accounting structure.
Actual Usage (consistent physical units): Physical product used during a period, calculated in consistent physical units. Do
not mix physical quantities with dollar values.
Approved House Recipe: The restaurant’s approved, costed, versioned, programmed, trained, and verified recipe
standard.
Backup Inventory: Additional approved inventory held to protect service from replenishment risk.
Bar Capability: The set of approved beverages the restaurant can reliably produce with its current ingredients, tools,
standards, and trained team.
Bar Supplies: Non-beverage operating supplies needed to execute beverage service. These remain financially separate
from beverage product purchases.
Batch Cocktail: A beverage recipe produced in a controlled larger quantity using an approved batch formula, version, and
yield.
Beverage Attachment Rate: Relevant guest checks with the target beverage divided by relevant guest checks, multiplied
by 100.
Beverage Cost %: Beverage product cost divided by beverage sales, multiplied by 100.
Beverage Master Standard Register: The central controlled record of approved beverage standards, costs, prices,
training/POS verification, inventory classification, and current status.
Bottle Equivalent: A standard full-bottle quantity used to express partial or open-bottle inventory.
Bottle-to-Drink Index: A controlled map showing every approved sellable drink a bottle or ingredient supports.
Breakage: Documented product or container loss caused by breakage and recorded under the restaurant’s approved loss-
control process.
Comp: A manager-approved complimentary beverage or adjustment recorded in the POS and supporting records
according to restaurant policy.
Contribution Dollars: Selling price minus recipe cost.
Cost Change Register: A controlled record of previous cost, new cost, effective date, percentage change, affected
products, and management action.
Cost per Ounce: Bottle cost divided by bottle ounces.
Dead Inventory: Product that is not supporting sufficient approved demand, menu reach, or operating purpose to justify
continued holding.
Deplete (D): Use existing inventory but do not reorder without new approval.
Documented Adjustment: An approved recorded change explaining product that legitimately moved outside normal sales
usage.
Drink-to-Ingredient Index: A controlled map showing the ingredients, modifiers, garnishes, and specialty items required by
each approved drink.
Essential (E): Core inventory required by the approved beverage program.
Event Adjustment: A documented temporary change to inventory, target, or order quantity for a defined event, holiday,
reservation, promotion, or other approved exception.
Expanded (X): Specialty or expanded-capability inventory justified by demand and contribution.
Extended Cost: Quantity multiplied by unit cost.
Final Order: Suggested order after documented, approved management adjustments.
Full-Service (F): Inventory that supports a broader full-service beverage program.
Gravity Bar Capability Matrix: The controlled tool used to verify what the bar can reliably produce based on inventory,
equipment, training, POS readiness, and operating standards.
Gravity Baseline Recipe: A practical starting recipe standard that may be replaced by a restaurant’s approved house
version.
Gravity Fulfillment: Continuing-use replacement or refill materials that help an installed Gravity process keep operating
without requiring the customer to repurchase the master product.
Gravity Smart Order: The controlled ordering method that begins with Target Need and Usable Inventory On Hand, then
documents any approved management adjustment before the Final Order is placed.
THE GRAVITY SYSTEM                                                                                                        299

Inventory Classification: The approved assignment of beverage product to Essential, Full-Service, or Expanded inventory,
plus any applicable Seasonal, Test, Deplete, or Review status.
Inventory Value: Quantity on hand multiplied by unit cost.
Manager Adjustment: A documented management change to the mathematically Suggested Order based on an approved
operating reason.
Manager on Duty (MOD): The designated leader with authority for the operating shift or daypart according to the
restaurant’s management structure.
Menu Reach: The number of approved sellable drinks an ingredient supports.
One-Bottle Expansion Test: A controlled test used before adding a bottle to determine whether menu reach, expected
demand, contribution, and complexity justify the inventory.
One-More-Ingredient Test: A controlled test used before adding an ingredient to determine whether the added menu
capability justifies shelf-life, waste, prep, training, and inventory complexity.
Open-Bottle Equivalent: The estimated fraction of a full bottle remaining in an open bottle.
Operating Supply Purchase: A purchase of non-beverage supplies used to support bar service. It is tracked separately
and is not included in beverage product cost.
Par: The approved normal inventory level.
Permanent Inventory: Inventory approved for normal ongoing operation rather than a temporary Seasonal or Test period.
Physical Ending Inventory: The actual physical quantity counted at the end of the measurement period.
POS (Point of Sale): The system used to record beverage sales, prices, modifiers, premiums, comps, discounts, and
related transaction data.
POS Verification: Confirmation that the POS item, price, modifier, recipe-related programming, and approved operating
standard agree.
Pour Cost: The product cost associated with the approved quantity poured into a beverage.
Pour Standard: The approved quantity and measurement method for a controlled beverage pour.
Purchase Unit: The defined vendor unit in which a product is ordered, delivered, invoiced, and converted to the
restaurant’s inventory unit when necessary.
Recipe Cost: The sum of all measured ingredient costs in an approved recipe.
Recipe Version: The controlled version identifier assigned to an approved recipe.
Receiving Verification: The controlled comparison of the approved order, delivered product/quantity/condition,
substitutions, and invoice cost before receiving is accepted.
Reorder Status: The current management decision governing whether a product should be reordered, held, tested,
depleted, or reviewed.
Review (R): Management review required before the next purchase or permanent-status decision.
Seasonal (S): Temporary inventory approved for a defined seasonal period.
SKU (Stock-Keeping Unit): A distinct product or purchasing item treated as its own inventory unit.
Suggested Order: Target Need minus Usable Inventory On Hand, converted to practical purchase units as appropriate.
Target Need: The applicable approved inventory target used for the current order: normally Par, or a Temporary Target
when an approved exception is active.
Temporary Target: A time-limited inventory level approved for an event or exception. It does not permanently change Par.
Test (T): Inventory held for a controlled trial.
Theoretical Beverage Cost: The product cost that should have been used based on approved recipe costs and recorded
sales.
Theoretical Beverage Cost %: Theoretical Beverage Cost divided by beverage sales, multiplied by 100.
Theoretical Usage (same physical units): Approved recipe quantity multiplied by recorded units sold, aggregated across
every applicable beverage using the product.
Transfer In: Product moved into the controlled inventory area from another authorized location.
Transfer Out: Product moved out of the controlled inventory area to another authorized location.
Unit Cost: The cost of one defined purchase or inventory unit.
Unexplained Variance: The remaining difference after known, documented causes have been evaluated.
Usage Variance (Actual − Theoretical): Actual Usage minus Theoretical Usage in the same physical unit. A positive
variance means Actual Usage exceeded Theoretical Usage.
Usable Inventory On Hand: Physical On Hand minus unusable or committed product.
Vendor Credit: Money or account value owed by a vendor because of an approved shortage, return, pricing correction,
damage, or other discrepancy.

Responsibility & Verification Standard
Every recurring beverage-control task should answer: Who does it? Who verifies it? When is it due? What happens when
something is wrong?
Gravity assigns responsibility by role/function rather than assuming every restaurant has the same job titles. Where
complete separation of duties is impractical, use compensating verification rather than abandoning control.
